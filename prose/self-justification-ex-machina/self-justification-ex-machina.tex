\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Sebastian Oberhoff's essay \emph{Incompleteness Ex Machina} proves Gödel's
incompleteness theorems twice, once with sentences and once with programs,
and concludes that Gödel's work divides into two parts: teaching arithmetic
to compute, and the incompleteness theorems, which are victory laps. We look
closely at the one step of the second theorem that Oberhoff takes ``with some
faith''. It asks a formal system to believe that whenever it can watch a
program halt, it can also write down a transcript of the run, and transcripts
are bigger than what they record. That belief is a claim about how fast
numbers grow. Dan Willard's self-justifying axiom systems are what remains
when the belief is withheld. They watch computations as well as any
arithmetic does, they are incomplete, and they take their own consistency as
an axiom and are right. We retell Willard's consistency argument as a program
in Oberhoff's style, show that the faith step cannot be proved in such a
system (Oberhoff's own argument, run backwards), and locate where the faith
comes back: at multiplication for proofs that never reuse a lemma, and
already at counting for proofs that do. Gödel's work, rightly viewed, splits
into three parts, not two.
\end{abstract}

\begin{quote}\small
Another way to motivate the axiom system IS is to consider a philosopher's
response to the question of whether multiplication is a function. His first
answer would likely be ``of course yes''. But if one then asked the
philosopher to construct a sequence of natural numbers
$a_0, a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots$ where $a_0 \ge 2$ and $a_{i+1} = (a_i)^2$, a hedge
would quickly follow because $a_n$ requires at least $2^n$ bits. The expected
response would then be that ``multiplication is a function in theory but not
always in practice''.
\hfill --- Dan Willard, SUNY Albany TR~93-10, p.~6
\end{quote}

\section{Victory Laps}

Oberhoff's essay~\cite{oberhoff} does something deceptively simple. It
writes out Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, Rosser's improvement of it,
and the second incompleteness theorem as explicit case analyses. Then it
replaces Gödel's sentence $\mathsf{G}$ (``I am not provable'') by
$\G = \fs{$\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ doesn't halt}$, where $\Prog{P}$ is the program
\begin{lstlisting}
P(A) {
  for s $\in$ all possible strings {
    if s is a proof in $\F$ that A(A) doesn't halt {
      halt;
    }
  }
}
\end{lstlisting}
and observes that each case analysis can be copied almost word for word.
The punchline (his \S5) is that $\G$ is $\mathsf{G}$ wearing Clark Kent's
glasses: $\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ is searching $\F$'s proofs for a proof of $\G$,
so $\G$ says ``I am not provable''. His moral (\S6) is a refactoring. The
hard part of Gödel's work was teaching arithmetic to compute, packaged as
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Theorem 7} (Turing Completeness of Elementary Arithmetic).
\emph{Every formal system capable of reasoning about elementary arithmetic
can also reason about algorithms,}
\end{quote}
which Oberhoff leaves ``to smarter people''. The incompleteness theorems
themselves are victory laps.

For the first incompleteness theorem we accept this completely. This essay
is about the second. Its proof in~\cite{oberhoff} contains exactly one step
that Oberhoff marks as taken on trust (Theorem~6, third step):
\begin{quote}
Now we simply observe (with some faith) that there wasn't actually anything
in the reasoning we used to prove this that we couldn't carry out just as
well in $\F$.
\end{quote}
For the systems Oberhoff has in mind, that faith is well placed. But some
formal systems can reason about algorithms in every sense the first theorem
needs, and for them the faith is misplaced and the conclusion of Theorem~6 is
false: they prove their own consistency, and they are consistent. Dan Willard
constructed them between 1993 and 2021~\cite{w93tr,w01,w05,w06a,w20}. (We
cite Willard's papers by year, as in the bibliography: ``2002a'' is
\cite{w02a}.) This essay tells their story in Oberhoff's language. Our headline is that the
faith step is not part of the victory lap. It is a third part of Gödel's
work, and it has a price.

\section{Where the Faith Lives}
\label{sec:faith}

Here is the first half of Oberhoff's proof that $\G$ is undecidable (his \S4,
a second proof of his Theorem~4):

\Case{Suppose $\G$ is provable:}
\SubCase{Or $\neg\G$ isn't provable:}
\begin{steps}
\item Because $\G$ is provable $\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ will eventually find such
  a proof after some sequence of steps which we can write out in $\F$.
\item Because this sequence leads to a terminal state this gives a proof of
  $\fs{$\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ halts} = \neg\G$.\contra
\end{steps}

\smallskip\noindent
Theorem~6 asks $\F$ to run this argument on itself. Written as statements
that $\F$ must prove, the argument has three steps.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*), leftmargin=2.5em]
\item \fs{If $\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ halts, then $\G$ is provable.} This is
  cheap. $\Prog{P}$ halts only after it has found a proof of $\G$. If we
  formalize \fs{$\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ halts} as \fs{$\Prog{P}$'s search
  succeeds}, which is how arithmetic usually formalizes it, the two sentences
  are the same sentence.\footnote{If halting is formalized through execution
  traces instead, even this step costs something: given a proof $s$, $\F$
  must believe that the trace of the search up to $s$ exists. Willard always
  works with proof predicates directly.}
\item \fs{If $\G$ is provable, then $\neg\G$ is provable.} This is the step
  ``$\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ will eventually find such a proof after some
  sequence of steps which we can write out in $\F$''. Inside $\F$ it reads:
  \emph{for every proof $s$ of $\G$ there is a proof of
  \fs{$\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ halts}}, namely the proof that exhibits $s$ and
  checks it.
\item \fs{If $\G$ and $\neg\G$ are both provable, then $\F$ is inconsistent.}
  This is free if ``consistent'' means that no sentence is provable together
  with its negation. If it means that there is no proof of $0=1$, then $\F$
  must also believe that two proofs can be combined into a third. That is
  cheap with modus ponens and expensive without it (\S\ref{sec:price}).
\end{enumerate}
Chaining (1)--(3) gives $\F \vdash \fs{$\neg\G \Rightarrow \F$ is
inconsistent}$, that is, $\F \vdash \fs{$\F$ is consistent $\Rightarrow \G$}$,
which is what Theorem~6 needs.

The only step that asks $\F$ to know something substantial about itself is
(2). Look at its shape. In the first incompleteness theorem, ``which we can
write out in $\F$'' was a fact \emph{about} $\F$, established by us from
outside: we are handed a halting run and we produce the proof. In the second
theorem it has to be a theorem \emph{of} $\F$, stated for every $s$ at once.
The two deserve separate names.

\begin{definition}[Watching]
$\F$ \emph{watches} algorithms if, whenever a program halts, $\F$ proves
that it halts.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Knowing that one watches]
$\F$ \emph{knows that it watches} if $\F$ proves, for programs $\Prog{A}$,
\fs{if $\Prog{A}$ halts, then $\F$ proves that $\Prog{A}$ halts}.
\end{definition}

Watching is external $\Sigma_1$-completeness. Knowing that one watches is
formalized $\Sigma_1$-completeness, in computational dress; the third
Hilbert--Bernays--Löb derivability condition~\cite{hb39,lob55}, which the
classical proof of the second theorem uses, is the special case in which the
program is a proof search. Here is what
each result of~\cite{oberhoff} uses:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llccc@{}}
\toprule
Result in~\cite{oberhoff} & Assumes about $\F$ & Watches & Knows it watches \\
\midrule
Thm 4 (G1 via halting) & honest; complete (for contradiction) & --- & --- \\
\S4, $\G$ undecidable & consistent; honest in case 2 & case 1 & --- \\
Thm 5 (Rosser, via $\Prog{B}$) & consistent & both cases & --- \\
Thm 6 (G2) & consistent & yes & step (2) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
Theorem 4 needs only that $\F$ can \emph{state} halting facts honestly; the
search does the rest. Rosser's case 2 also uses the fact that a program which
has entered \texttt{while true \{\}} never leaves it, a $\Pi_1$ fact that
Oberhoff's footnote~12 says every respectable system can prove. Apart from
that, every result in the essay except the second incompleteness theorem uses
watching and nothing more. That is no accident of presentation. The
classical proof of the second theorem runs through the derivability
conditions; the computational retelling shows what the third condition
\emph{says}. It says that transcripts exist.

\section{Transcripts Are Bigger Than Runs}
\label{sec:transcripts}

Why should knowing be harder than watching? Because of a size mismatch. A
proof of \fs{$\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ halts} has to contain $s$, or at least name
it, and check it step by step. Written out, the check is bigger than $s$.
(Replaying $\Prog{P}$'s whole search up to $s$ would be far bigger still.) So
step~(2) asks $\F$ to prove a statement of the form ``for every $s$ there
exists $t$'', where $t$ is a number larger than $s$, obtained from $s$
uniformly. That is a totality claim: $\F$ must believe that the function
taking a proof to its checked transcript is defined everywhere. A totality
claim about a function that grows is a claim about how big numbers get.

Most arithmetics accept it without blinking. Willard noticed that one can
refuse it without refusing to compute. His first statement of the idea
continues this essay's epigraph (TR 93-10, p.~7, verbatim):
\begin{quote}\small
This means there will always be adequate memory for writing $b_n$'s binary
encoding when there is sufficient memory for storing a semantic tableaux
proof of its existence (whereas the same is plainly not true \emph{when
multiplication is assumed to be a function}).

For instance, multiplication allows one to establish the existence of a
number $a_{300}$ (in a proof with approximately 300 lines), whose binary
encoding requires \emph{more digits} than the number of atoms in the
universe.
\end{quote}
Here $b_{i+1} = b_i + b_i$ and $a_{i+1} = (a_i)^2$. The arithmetic is simple.
Start both sequences at~2:
\[
x_n = 2^{n+1} \;(\text{doubling}), \qquad
y_n = 2^{2^n} \;(\text{squaring}), \qquad
\log_2 x_n = n+1, \qquad \log_2 y_n = 2^n .
\]
A proof that builds $x_n$ by $n$ doublings has about $n$ steps, and $x_n$
has about $n$ binary digits: the number fits in the memory its proof
occupies. A proof that builds $y_n$ by $n$ squarings also has about $n$
steps, but $y_n$ has $2^n$ digits. Willard's later statement is sharper. It
compares the Gödel number of a proof with the integer the proof shows to
exist (Willard 2006a, Remark~3, p.~24, verbatim):
\begin{quote}\small
In particular, every proof of the existence of an integer $N$ using the
additive convention will have a larger Gödel number than $N$. In contrast,
the multiplicative convention allows for the Gödel number of such proofs to
have a sharply smaller magnitude than $N$ (when for instance
$N = 2^{2^k}$ and $k$ is larger than some fixed constant). In essence, this
difference in magnitude is the reason the Second Incompleteness Theorem will
generalize under the multiplicative naming paradigm --- while Section 4
showed boundary-case exceptions to it exist under the additive naming
convention.
\end{quote}

\centering

Put the two observations together. Step~(2) asks for transcripts, which
are bigger than what they transcribe. In a system whose proofs cannot
establish the existence of numbers bigger than themselves, step~(2) is in
trouble. For now this is a heuristic; \S\ref{sec:backwards} turns it into a
theorem, and the sections between describe systems built so that it holds.

\section{A Machine That Says ``I Am Consistent''}
\label{sec:genius}

Oberhoff's moral for the second theorem is ``if you declare yourself a very
stable genius, you're not''. Let us build the genius with his tools and watch
it fall. Fix an honest base system $\Fo$ that can reason about algorithms,
and consider

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}
C(A) {
  for s $\in$ all possible strings {
    if s is a refutation of $\Fo + \fs{A(A) doesn't halt}$ {
      halt;
    }
  }
}
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}
A \emph{refutation} of a system is a proof of $0=1$ from it. (We picked
$\Prog{C}$ because it is a circle that never quite closes; \S\ref{sec:machine}
explains the joke.) Let
\[
\W = \fs{$\Prog{C}(\Prog{C})$ doesn't halt}, \qquad \F = \Fo + \W .
\]
Just as $\G$ says ``I am not provable'', $\W$ says ``nothing refutes $\Fo$
together with me''. In other words, $\W$ says that $\F$ is consistent. So $\F$
proves its own consistency in one line, because the claim is an axiom. This
is the self-referential axiom of Willard's systems, built (like $\G$) by the
recursion theorem. Willard (2016, Definition~3.4 and Example~3.5)
remarks that constructing it is ``relatively easy, via an application of the
Fixed Point Theorem, but this sentence is ironically typically useless!''

The uselessness takes one line to prove.

\begin{proposition}[The genius falls]
Let $\Fo$ be Peano Arithmetic, or any other system strong enough that every
effectively axiomatized extension of it knows that it watches. Then
$\F = \Fo + \W$ is inconsistent, and $\Prog{C}(\Prog{C})$ halts.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $\F$ is consistent.
\begin{topsteps}
\item $\F$ proves its own consistency, since $\W$ is one of its axioms.
\item $\F$ satisfies every hypothesis of Oberhoff's Theorem~6, including the
  faith step.
\item So $\F$ cannot prove its own consistency.\contra
\end{topsteps}
So $\F$ is inconsistent. A refutation exists (it is Theorem~6's argument,
written out in $\F$), and $\Prog{C}(\Prog{C})$ finds it.
\end{proof}

Willard records that ``Kleene, Rogers and Jeroslow [\ldots] each warned their readers
that most axiom systems, similar to $\alpha^*$, were useless on account of
their inconsistency, although they were technically well-defined''
(2005, \S1). With faith, the genius falls on the first day.

\section{Willard's Machine}
\label{sec:machine}

Willard keeps $\Prog{C}$ and $\W$ and changes $\Fo$. In the version that is
easiest to explain, the tableaux line of 2001--2005, $\Fo$ has three groups
of axioms and one restriction on proofs (codified statement~\S5.1):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \textbf{Names.} Constants for $0$, $1$, $2$, with addition total. In the
  2005 version, addition and $\mathit{Double}(x) = x + x$ are function
  symbols, so every number $n$ has a name of $O(\log n)$ symbols.
  Multiplication is \emph{not} a function. It is a three-place relation
  $M(x,y,z)$, and $\Fo$ does not prove $\forall x \forall y \exists z\,
  M(x,y,z)$. (Multiplication by any \emph{fixed} constant remains total; what
  is withheld is uniform multiplication.)
\item \textbf{Watching.} A finite set of $\Pi_1$ axioms that proves every true
  bounded ($\Delta_0$) sentence and gives $=$ and $\le$ their usual
  properties. ``This finite string is a halting run of $\Prog{A}$'' is a
  bounded sentence, so $\Fo$ watches every halting program.
\item \textbf{Inheritance.} For each $\Pi_1$ sentence $\Phi$, the axiom
  \fs{for every $y$, if $y$ is a proof of $\Phi$ in $A$, then $\Phi$}, for a
  chosen base theory $A$ such as Peano Arithmetic. Because $\Fo$ watches,
  it can verify any particular $A$-proof, and so it proves every $\Pi_1$
  theorem of $A$. That includes every fact of the form ``this program never
  halts'' that $A$ knows, footnote~12's \texttt{while true \{\}} among them.
\item \textbf{Proofs without lemmas.} The proof system is semantic tableaux:
  proof by refutation tree, with no cut rule and no modus ponens. (Willard
  2005 later admits lemmas, but only $\Pi_1$ and $\Sigma_1$ ones: subroutine
  calls with a very simple interface.)
\end{itemize}

\begin{theorem}[Willard]
If the $\Pi_1$ theorems of $A$ are true, then $\F = \Fo + \W$ is consistent.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
This is Willard 2001, Theorem~4.3 (p.~20), for the system $\mathrm{IS}(A)$,
whose $\W$ forbids proofs of $0=1$; and Willard 2005, Theorems~3 and~5
(pp.~27, 29), for $\mathrm{IS}_D(A)$, whose $\W$ forbids proving any $\Pi_1$
sentence together with its negation, under tableaux and under tableaux with
$\Pi_1$/$\Sigma_1$ lemmas respectively. Both proofs are complete
(status~\texttt{full}). Like every consistency proof, this is a theorem of
the metatheory, proved under a hypothesis (here, that $A$'s $\Pi_1$ theorems
are true, and that $A$'s proofs are encoded in the standard bounded way); it
is a relative consistency result.

By Oberhoff's standards, then, $\F$ is a formal system capable of reasoning
about algorithms. It watches, and it proves every $\Pi_1$ theorem of Peano
Arithmetic. It is honest: its base axioms are true, its added axiom $\W$ is
$\Pi_1$, and a consistent extension of a true, $\Sigma_1$-complete base by
$\Pi_1$ axioms proves only truths.\footnote{A false $\Pi_1$ axiom would have
a true $\Sigma_1$ negation, which the base proves, so the extension would be
inconsistent. See the repository note
\texttt{docs/log/2026-09-24-soundness-of-willard-sjas.md}.} It is
incomplete: Oberhoff's Theorem~5 goes through verbatim, because Rosser's
program $\Prog{B}$ is watched in both cases and the loop fact is a $\Pi_1$
theorem of Peano Arithmetic. Willard said as much in his first technical
report: $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ is ``a class of structures, lying halfway between
Peano Arithmetic and Presburger Arithmetic, which satisfy Godel's First but
not Second Incompleteness Theorem'', because the predicate
$\mathit{Turing}(x,y,z)$ is expressible in it (TR 93-10, p.~6). And yet $\F$
proves its own consistency.

Here is why it is consistent, in Oberhoff's format. This is Willard's own
sketch (2001, Remark~4.4, pp.~20--21), put in computational words.

\Case{Suppose $\F$ is inconsistent:}
\begin{steps}
\item Then $\Prog{C}(\Prog{C})$ halts. There exists some shortest refutation
  $y$.
\item $y$ is a tableau, a tree in which every branch closes. Walk down $y$,
  always choosing a branch on which everything said so far is true of the
  numbers named so far. Everything $\Fo$ says is true, so only $\W$ can stop
  the walk.
\item $\W$ says \fs{no $s$ is a refutation of $\F$}. A tableau uses a
  universal axiom only by instantiating it at a term the branch has already
  built. So $\W$ can stop the walk only at a number named on the branch that
  actually bounds a refutation of $\F$.
\item But $y$ can name only numbers smaller than itself. Its fastest
  operation is doubling, every doubling costs symbols, and $y$ has too few
  symbols to double its way past its own Gödel number.
\item So $\F$ has a refutation smaller than $y$, which was the shortest
  one.\contra
\end{steps}

\smallskip\noindent
Hence $\F$ is consistent. Since $\W$ says exactly that, $\W$ is true, and
$\F$ is honest.

Willard compresses the argument into one sentence (2001, p.~20, verbatim):
``any proof $y$ of $0=1$ from $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ will be automatically
associated with an ordered pair $(p,u)$ where
$p \le \mathrm{VALUE}(u) < y$ and $p$ represents ANOTHER proof of $0=1$''.
He calls this a \emph{vacuous truth}, an implication whose hypothesis is never
satisfied, and notes that once it is established ``the remainder of the proof
of Theorem 4.3 is fairly routine and easy''. All the work is in the fourth
arrow. The formal proof replaces the walk by an eleven-case induction over
the tableau rules (Lemma~4.8, p.~23, \texttt{full}). The induction shows that
the least refutation contains an ``affirmative'' branch, and such a branch
cannot close (Lemma~4.6, p.~22, \texttt{full}). The naming bound comes from
Lemma~4.1 (a constant mentioned in $y$ has value below $\sqrt{y}$, p.~17,
\texttt{full}) and Lemma~4.2 (along such a branch every parameter has value
below $y$, p.~19, \texttt{sketch}). In numbers: there are fewer than
$\tfrac13 \log_2 y$ doublings, so values reach at most
$y^{1/2} \cdot y^{1/3} = y^{5/6} < y$. That is Figure~\ref{fig:horizon}.

Three remarks.

\emph{The proof is a program.} The walk is an algorithm, later named
$\mathit{Probe}$ (Willard 2005, Lemma~1, p.~23, \texttt{full}). It takes any
purported refutation and returns an open branch, together with values for its
variables that make every sentence on the branch true. Willard's consistency
proof is therefore a machine that defeats each refutation by running it, in a
universe barely larger than the refutation itself. That is self-justification
ex machina in the most literal sense. (And it is why we named the searcher
$\Prog{C}$: it hunts for a tree all of whose branches close, and the walk
always finds one that stays open.) $\F$ cannot prove that the machine works;
that is the external part. $\F$ has only the axiom.

\emph{The encoding is part of the argument.} The horizon is measured in Gödel
numbers, so it depends on how proofs are written. In Willard's 2001
accounting, every addition can double a value, and the margin absorbs this
only because writing down the sentence that introduces the sum ``requires
substantially more than six bits'' (2001, p.~28). In the 2005 version the
coding density $1/5$ (Eq.~20, six-bit bytes) enters the bound directly. A
proof notation compact enough to let a proof name numbers larger than itself
would break the argument. Encoding choices are not bookkeeping here; they
drive self-justification. (Willard notes that on the Hilbert line his results
survive uncompressed encodings, though less meaningfully. Density is
load-bearing for the tableaux line in particular.)

\emph{There are two horizons.} The 2005 version, with $\W$ forbidding a
$\Pi_1$ sentence $\Psi$ and its negation, replaces the walk with a
quantitative statement. Take a minimal pair of proofs of $\Psi$ and of
$\neg\Psi$, and let $m$ be the larger. Below $m$, $\W$ is true. Willard's
\emph{$\theta$-compactification} says that a proof of length $p$ can stretch
a sentence's reach by at most a factor $p^{\theta}$, and tableaux achieve
$\theta = 1/4$ (2005, Definition~5 and Theorem~2, pp.~19, 26,
\texttt{full}). So the proof of the universal sentence $\Psi$ certifies it
out to $(m-1)/m^{1/4}$, while the proof of the existential $\neg\Psi$ finds
its counterexample below $m^{1/4}$. For $m \ge 3$ the counterexample lies
where $\Psi$ has been certified.\contra{} (Theorem~1, p.~19,
\texttt{full}. The printed proof treats the closing inequality as immediate;
it needs $m \ge 3$, which always holds for the Gödel number of a proof.) A
proof of ``always'' guarantees its claim out to its horizon, and a proof of
``sometimes'' finds its witness inside its horizon. When horizons are short
compared with the proofs, the two collide in the region where every axiom is
true.

\section{The Faith Is Not Just Missing}
\label{sec:backwards}

One might think Willard's systems are self-justifying merely because nobody
has managed to prove step~(2) in them. In fact they cannot prove it. The
reason is Oberhoff's Theorem~6, run backwards.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:backwards}
Let $\F$ be consistent and effectively axiomatized. Suppose $\F$ watches,
proves that no $\Pi_1$ sentence is provable in $\F$ together with its
negation, and proves the fixed-point equivalence
$\G \leftrightarrow \neg\Prov_{\F}(\ulcorner\G\urcorner)$ for its own $\G$.
Then $\F$ does not prove step~(2):
\[
\F \nvdash \Prov_{\F}(\ulcorner\G\urcorner) \Rightarrow
\Prov_{\F}(\ulcorner\neg\G\urcorner).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $\F$ proves step~(2).
\begin{topsteps}
\item $\F$ proves step~(1), which is half of the fixed-point equivalence.
  $\F$ proves step~(3), because $\G$ is a $\Pi_1$ sentence and step~(3) is an
  instance of $\F$'s self-consistency.
\item Chaining (1)--(3): $\F \vdash \neg\G \Rightarrow \bot$, so
  $\F \vdash \G$. (Theorems are closed under logical consequence for every
  complete proof system, with or without cut~\cite{w20}.)
\item So $\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ finds this proof and halts.
\item $\F$ watches $\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ halt: $\F \vdash \neg\G$. So $\F$ is
  inconsistent.\contra
\end{topsteps}
\end{proof}

Willard's $\mathrm{IS}_D(A)$ satisfies every hypothesis except possibly the
last (2005, Eq.~6 and Theorems~3 and~5). The last, the fixed-point
equivalence, is assumed here as for Willard's own self-referential axiom; for $A \supseteq$
Peano Arithmetic, the uniqueness facts about the substitution function that
it needs are true $\Pi_1$ theorems of $A$ and are therefore inherited. Hence
\emph{a consistent system that proves its own consistency cannot know that it
can watch its own proof-searcher halt.} Step~(2) is in fact true in such a
system, since its antecedent is false ($\G$ is unprovable), so it is a true
sentence that the system cannot prove. Oberhoff's faith step is the system's
own blind spot. For systems whose $\W$ only forbids proofs of $0=1$, such as
the 2001 $\mathrm{IS}(A)$, step~(3) also costs something (combining two
proofs into a refutation needs a cut), so the faith fails in at least one of
two places.

The proposition is a contrapositive and claims no novelty. Willard's own 1994
framing already sets the alternatives out: ``every axiom system either fails
to prove its consistency or fails to meet the full Hilbert-Bernays
conditions'' (TR 93-10, p.~6). What the computational reading adds is
\emph{which} condition fails, at \emph{which} sentence, and the reason it
fails. What Willard adds, and what no contrapositive can supply, is that
systems on the far side of the alternative \emph{exist}, and are strong
enough to prove every $\Pi_1$ theorem of Peano Arithmetic.

\section{The Price of Faith}
\label{sec:price}

If withholding faith is what lets $\W$ survive, the second incompleteness
theorem should return as soon as the system has enough growth to know that
transcripts exist. Willard spent the second half of his career proving that
it does, and pricing the faith exactly. The price depends on the proof
system. The difference is the difference between a program that may call
subroutines and one that may not.

\subsection*{Squaring}

Give the tableaux system multiplication as a total function. Then a proof can introduce
$u_0 = 2$, $u_{i+1} = u_i \cdot u_i$, and so reach $u_n = 2^{2^n}$, in $O(n)$
steps. Proofs
can now establish the existence of numbers far larger than themselves, so
transcripts fit, and the walk of \S\ref{sec:machine} fails at its fourth
arrow. Willard located the failure precisely (2000, \S5, quoted in the
codified statement~\S5.3):
\begin{quote}\small
Many readers will smile with amusement when they learn the true
reason that the Semantic Tableaux version of the Second Incompleteness
Theorem breaks down when Multiplication is changed from a total function into
a 3-way relation. It is essentially that Lemma 8 and its short proof
then become no longer valid. \ldots{} Without it, our proof of the Semantic
Tableaux version of the Second Incompleteness Theorem collapses entirely!
\end{quote}
Lemma~8 is the squaring construction. With it, the second theorem comes back.
No consistent finite extension of $Q + V$, for an explicit $\Pi_1$ sentence
$V$, proves its own tableaux consistency (2002a, Theorem~3.5, p.~11,
\texttt{full}). The same holds for every consistent extension of the
textbook and Wilkie--Paris axiomatizations of $I\Sigma_0$ (2007, Theorem~6,
p.~29, \texttt{full}), and, under a technical condition on how a system recognizes its own
axioms, for extensions of $I\Sigma_0$ generally (2002a, Theorem~6.4, p.~25,
\texttt{sketch}; this answers a 1981 question of Paris and Wilkie). From the positive side, the compactification engine becomes
``impossible to satisfy'' once multiplication is admitted as a ninth function
symbol (2005, Remark~4, p.~21).\footnote{One nuance, to keep the boundary
honest. What multiplication destroys is the flat consistency axiom and
canonical reflection. Willard's $\mathrm{ISM}(A)$ recognizes multiplication
as total and is still consistent, by what Willard calls a routine
generalization of his proof; but its self-referential axiom is weaker, and it
cannot prove the flat statement that no proof of $0=1$ exists (2001, \S6;
codified statement~\S7.2).}

\subsection*{Subroutines}

A Hilbert-style proof can prove a lemma once and use it many times. Modus
ponens is a subroutine call. A tableau cannot do this; every use must be
inlined. Inlining can be astronomically expensive: cut elimination preserves
theorems, but the cut-free proof ``can certainly be super-exponentially
longer'' (2002a, Theorem~2.2, p.~7), and Boolos gave a famous small example
of the blowup~\cite{boolos84}. With subroutines, a little growth is
amplified into a lot. Willard saw this in 1994 (TR 93-10, p.~7, verbatim):
\begin{quote}\small
These metaphors lose meaning when cuts are present because Addition can then
be employed to \emph{implicitly define} a sequence that grows actually faster
than $a_0, a_1, a_2, a_3 \ldots$. Solovay's proof that IS(A) is incompatible
with deductive cuts [So94] rests on this fast growth property. (Actually,
IS(A) can support a cut rule for $\Delta_0$ sentences, and Solovay's theorem
relies on the cut rule for sentences with unbounded quantifiers.)
\end{quote}
In outline, the mechanism is the classical shortening of definable cuts.
With lemmas, one defines a set of numbers and proves once that it is closed
under successor; then one proves, by a short proof reusing that lemma, that a
smaller set is closed under addition, and then that a smaller set still is
closed under multiplication. On the last set the system, roughly speaking, knows that
transcripts exist, and Gödel's argument runs there: Pudlák showed that a
consistent extension of Robinson's $Q$ cannot prove even its consistency
restricted to a definable cut~\cite{pudlak85} (Willard 2006a, Theorem~1,
p.~4, \texttt{cited}). Solovay's
theorem, communicated to Willard by telephone in April 1994 and never
published by Solovay, states the conclusion (Willard 2006a, Theorem~2, p.~4,
\texttt{cited}): no consistent system that proves successor total, together
with the basic algebra of $+$ and $\times$, can prove its own Hilbert
consistency. With subroutines, the price of faith is mere counting.

So the Hilbert-line self-justifying systems give up successor itself. They
name numbers with constants $C_1, C_2, \ldots$, and the naming convention
\emph{is} the system (codified statement~\S5.5): incremental naming
$C_i = C_{i-1} + 1$ works ($\mathrm{ISREF}(A)$, 2001, Theorem~3.4, p.~13,
\texttt{full}); additive naming $C_i = C_{i-1} + C_{i-1}$ works and keeps
reaching new numbers ($\mathrm{ISCE}(A)$, 2006a, Theorem~3, p.~6,
\texttt{full}); multiplicative naming $C_i = C_{i-1} \cdot C_{i-1}$ fails
(2006a, Theorem~4, p.~7, \texttt{full}). Remark~3, quoted in
\S\ref{sec:transcripts}, explains why.

\subsection*{Other ways to buy faith}

Adding the law of excluded middle as logical axioms puts the cut back into
tableaux by the back door. The self-justifying system then becomes
automatically inconsistent whenever its base proves the usual algebraic laws
of $+$ and $\times$ (2020, Theorem~4.5, p.~13, \texttt{sketch}). Letting $\W$
say more about itself, namely consistency for a richer class of sentences
(Willard's Level $2^{+}$), brings the second theorem back for finite systems
(2004, Theorem~1, p.~6, \texttt{full}).

\section{Trust Within Reach}

A system \emph{trusts} its proofs if it proves reflection: \fs{if $\F$ proves
that $\Prog{A}$ never halts, then $\Prog{A}$ never halts}, for every
$\Pi_1$ claim at once. That is too much to ask. No consistent system of this
kind, proving the $\Pi_1$ theorems of (a version of) Peano Arithmetic, proves
canonical reflection for every $\Pi_1$ sentence (2001, Theorem~7.2, p.~38,
\texttt{full}); it is Löb's theorem in Willard's setting. What Willard's
systems can prove is \emph{tangibility reflection} (2001, Definition~1.2,
Eq.~2):
\[
\forall x\;\bigl\{\,[\exists y\, \Prf_\alpha(\ulcorner\Psi\urcorner, y)
\wedge \mathit{Tangible}(x)] \supset \Psi^{x}\,\bigr\},
\]
where $\Psi^x$ is $\Psi$ with its unbounded universal quantifiers bounded by
$x$. A number is tangible when it has room above it: some $v$ with
$x < v - 1$, or $x < v^{1/k}$. $\mathrm{ISREF}(A)$ proves the first form, given
a condition on proof sizes (2001, Theorem~3.5, p.~14, \texttt{full}), and the
tableaux system $\mathrm{IS}^{\lambda}(A)$ proves the second, under conditions
on $\lambda$, $k$ and $A$ (Theorem~6.1, p.~34, and Corollary~6.2, p.~35, both
\texttt{full}). In computational terms: \emph{I
trust my verdicts on every input for which there is memory to spare.} For a
$\Sigma_1$ claim the bound costs nothing. With $\Psi$ taken to be $0=1$,
tangibility reflection gives back the flat statement that no proof of $0=1$
exists (2001, p.~8).

% ==========================================================================
\section{Taking Off the Glasses}

Oberhoff's \S5 takes the glasses off $\G$ and finds ``I am not provable''
underneath. Take them off $\W$ and you find ``nothing refutes me'', that is,
``I am consistent''. Over Peano Arithmetic, $\W$ is refuted, and
$\Prog{C}(\Prog{C})$ finds the refutation: Theorem~6, written out. In
Willard's systems that refutation would have to establish the existence of a
refutation, a transcript-sized object, from inside a proof smaller than the
object. The snake cannot swallow its tail, because its mouth is smaller than
its body.

So self-reference is not where the trouble lies. The recursion theorem builds
$\W$ in Willard's systems as easily as in Peano Arithmetic. What costs
something is \emph{self-simulation}: running yourself in your head and writing
down the result. The second incompleteness theorem needs both. The first needs
only self-reference and watching, which is why Willard's systems obey it.

Oberhoff found that his classical and computational proofs were ``the exact
same''. The same holds here. Willard's arithmetic (a least Gödel number $y$,
constants below $\sqrt{y}$, fewer than $\tfrac13\log_2 y$ doublings) and the
computational picture (a shortest refutation, the memory a proof occupies, a
walk down the tree) are one argument. The arithmetic of Gödel numbers is the
bookkeeping of memory.

\section{Refactoring the Incompleteness Theorems, Again}

Oberhoff's Theorem~7 is two theorems.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Theorem 7a} (Watching). \emph{Every formal system capable of
reasoning about elementary arithmetic proves, of each halting computation,
that it halts.}

\textbf{Theorem 7b} (Knowing that one watches). \emph{Such a system proves
Theorem~7a about itself.}
\end{quote}

\noindent
Theorem~7a is enough for the first incompleteness theorem in all of
Oberhoff's forms. Theorem~7b is what Theorem~6 takes on faith, and it does
not follow from~7a. Willard's systems satisfy~7a and prove their own
consistency, so by Proposition~\ref{prop:backwards} they fail~7b. Read in
Willard's results, the cost of~7b depends on the proof system. Without
lemmas, the second incompleteness theorem returns with uniform
multiplication; with lemmas, it returns already with successor. In both
cases what returns is not always~7b itself but enough of it, a compressed
proof that a big enough number exists, for Gödel's argument to run.

So Gödel's work splits into three parts, not two. The first is programming
the integers (7a). The second is teaching the integers that they have been
programmed (7b). The third is the victory laps. Willard's self-justifying
systems live in the gap between the first and the second. The second
incompleteness theorem is a victory lap only for a runner who has already
paid for~7b, and the receipt, in Willard's accounting, reads: multiplication
if you never reuse a lemma, counting if you do.

\medskip
Moral of the story: you may declare yourself a very stable genius, provided
you cannot multiply. If you like to reuse your lemmas, you must also be
unable to count.

\section{What Self-Justification Does Not Buy}

Willard was more careful about the limits of his results than most of his
readers have been, and an honest retelling must carry his caveats.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \textbf{A reduced question.} Willard (2016, Remark~4.9, p.~13) splits
  Hilbert's question in two: can a system verify its own consistency in a
  robust sense, and can it appreciate its consistency in some reduced but not
  immaterial sense? The answer to the first is no, by Gödel, Hilbert--Bernays
  and Pudlák--Solovay. Everything above answers the second.
\item \textbf{A one-line proof.} A system that proves its consistency from
  its own axiom $\W$ has ``essentially a 1-line proof'' (2011, Remark~6.16b,
  p.~28), which is instinctive faith rather than proof-justification. The
  substantive justification lies outside: the theorem that $\mathit{Probe}$
  defeats every refutation, proved in the metatheory under the hypothesis
  that $A$'s $\Pi_1$ theorems are true.
\item \textbf{Almost cheating.} Willard raised the objection against
  himself: ``Is it not almost cheating when an axiom system verifies its own
  consistency by using $\oplus$'s formalized `I am consistent' axiom as an
  intermediate step, to verify its own consistency? After all, such a
  technique can verify its own consistency only in a technically purely
  legalistic sense'' (2016, \S8). His answer, \emph{Platonic Stability}, is
  that a thinking being needs no sophisticated justification of its own
  reasoning, only a formalism in which presuming its own consistency provably
  will not spin it into inconsistency.
\item \textbf{The first theorem stands.} Willard's systems are incomplete;
  they cannot decide $\G$ or $\R$.
\item \textbf{Interpretation versus source.} The vocabulary of watching and
  knowing, the program $\Prog{C}$, the horizon picture and
  Proposition~\ref{prop:backwards} are our reading, not Willard's
  formulations. His own words for the mechanism are ``adequate memory''
  (1994) and the comparison, in 2006a Remark~3, between a proof's Gödel
  number and the integer the proof shows to exist.
\end{itemize}

\medskip\noindent
{\small (The cake promised in Oberhoff's abstract is still on its way. We
cannot prove that it exists: it is one multiplication away.)}
