\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Sebastian Oberhoff's essay \emph{Incompleteness Ex Machina} proves Gödel's
incompleteness theorems twice, once with sentences and once with programs,
and concludes that Gödel's work divides into two parts: teaching arithmetic
to compute, and the incompleteness theorems, which are victory laps. We look
closely at the one step of the second theorem that Oberhoff takes ``with some
faith'': that a system which can watch any run of its own proof-searcher can
also prove, of the run that never happens, that it would have seen it.
Willard's self-justifying axiom systems are what remains when that step is
withheld. His 2005 system $\mathrm{IS}_D(A)$, for semantic tableaux and for
tableau lists with one-quantifier-block lemmas, proves every true
$\Sigma_1$-class sentence of its language and takes its own Level(1)
consistency as an axiom. Willard proved, in ordinary metamathematics, that
it is consistent whenever the $\Pi_1$-class theorems of its base theory are
true. His 2001 system $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ asserts the weaker Level(0$-$)
consistency and is consistent under the analogous hypothesis of regular
consistency. Under these hypotheses both systems are honest. They obey the
first incompleteness theorem when the base theory also proves the
$\Pi_1$-class theorems of Peano Arithmetic in the system's own language:
$\mathrm{PA}{+}$, with Willard's 2005 function symbols, or $\mathrm{PAX}$,
with his 2001 ones. We state everything at the
granularity self-justification needs (language, deduction method, consistency
notion, proof predicate, encoding), fixing the conventions Willard leaves
open, and retell his 2005 consistency proof as a program in Oberhoff's case
format. We then show that the faith step fails in two ways. In its uniform
form it fails for both systems for a plain reason: a proof from their
universal axioms can only produce witnesses that grow linearly in its
inputs, so they cannot even prove that every number has a written name. At
the Gödel sentence itself the faith step says that if the Gödel sentence is
provable, then so is the $\Sigma_1$ statement that the proof-searcher halts.
In a system that proves its own Level(1) consistency, with a correct bounded
proof predicate, our fixed point and a pairing of each sentence with its
negation, this step is provably equivalent to the Gödel sentence, hence
unprovable. Willard's 2005 system meets these conditions. Willard's 2005
Theorem~6 then shows the faith being bought: a bounded transcript form of
the step is a $\Pi_1$-class theorem of $\mathrm{PA}{+}$, and one axiom
asserting that a single large number exists makes the system inconsistent.
Its fourth step, as printed, confuses two sentences. We sketch a repair, so
at this granularity the theorem has the status of a sketch.
Gödel's work,
viewed this way, splits into three parts, not two.
\end{abstract}

\begin{quote}\small
Another way to motivate the axiom system IS is to consider a philosopher's
response to the question of whether multiplication is a function. His first
answer would likely be ``of course yes''. But if one then asked the
philosopher to construct a sequence of natural numbers
$a_0, a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots$ where $a_0 \ge 2$ and $a_{i+1} = (a_i)^2$, a hedge
would quickly follow because $a_n$ requires at least $2^n$ bits. The expected
response would then be that ``multiplication is a function in theory but not
always in practice''.
\hfill --- Dan Willard, SUNY Albany TR~93-10, p.~6
\end{quote}

\section{Victory Laps}

Oberhoff's essay~\cite{oberhoff} does something deceptively simple. It
writes out Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, Rosser's improvement of it,
and the second incompleteness theorem as explicit case analyses. Then it
replaces Gödel's sentence $\mathsf{G}$ (``I am not provable'') by
$\G = \fs{$\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ doesn't halt}$, where $\Prog{P}$ is the program
\begin{lstlisting}
P(A) {
  for s $\in$ all possible strings {
    if s is a proof in $\F$ that A(A) doesn't halt {
      halt;
    }
  }
}
\end{lstlisting}
and observes that each case analysis can be copied almost word for word.
The punchline (his \S5) is that $\G$ is $\mathsf{G}$ wearing Clark Kent's
glasses: $\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ is searching $\F$'s proofs for a proof of $\G$,
so $\G$ says ``I am not provable''. His moral (\S6) is a refactoring. The
hard part of Gödel's work was teaching arithmetic to compute, packaged as
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Theorem 7} (Turing Completeness of Elementary Arithmetic).
\emph{Every formal system capable of reasoning about elementary arithmetic
can also reason about algorithms,}
\end{quote}
which Oberhoff leaves ``to smarter people''. The incompleteness theorems
themselves are victory laps.

For the first incompleteness theorem we accept this completely. This essay
is about the second. Its proof in~\cite{oberhoff} contains exactly one step
that Oberhoff marks as taken on trust (Theorem~6, third step):
\begin{quote}
Now we simply observe (with some faith) that there wasn't actually anything
in the reasoning we used to prove this that we couldn't carry out just as
well in $\F$.
\end{quote}
For the systems Oberhoff has in mind, that faith is well placed. But some
formal systems can reason about algorithms in every sense the first theorem
needs, and for them the faith is misplaced: they prove their own consistency,
and, by theorems of Willard's proved in ordinary metamathematics, they are
consistent whenever their base theory has a bounded proof predicate and true
$\Pi_1$-class theorems. For 2001 this is Willard's ``regular consistency''
(2001, p.~12); Theorem~\ref{thm:willard} gives the two cases exactly. Dan
Willard constructed them in papers from 1993 to 2020~\cite{w93tr,w01,w05,w06a,w20}.
(We cite Willard's papers by year, as in the bibliography: ``2002a'' is
\cite{w02a}.) This essay tells their story in Oberhoff's language. Our
headline is that the faith step is not part of the victory lap. It is a third
part of Gödel's work, and it has a price.

\paragraph{The standard of precision.} Oberhoff's essay is a paper proof at
the granularity the second incompleteness theorem needs: for Peano
Arithmetic and its relatives, it does not matter which language, which proof
system, which notion of consistency or which Gödel numbering one picks, since
the system proves that the choices agree. Self-justification lives exactly
where those choices stop agreeing. Willard's systems are self-justifying for
one deduction method and inconsistent for another, consistent at one
consistency level and not at the next, and his proofs measure Gödel numbers
in bits. An exegesis that is to have the proof status of Oberhoff's must
therefore fix every one of these parameters, and say where a result depends
on them. Section~\ref{sec:conventions} fixes them. Throughout, ``Willard
proved'' means that the result is printed with a complete proof (status
\texttt{full}); other statuses are named where they occur. Results labelled
\emph{ours} are proved here.

\section{Conventions}
\label{sec:conventions}

\paragraph{Configurations.} Following Willard, a \emph{configuration}
$(\alpha, D)$ is an axiom system $\alpha$ together with a deduction method
$D$; $\alpha \vdash_D \varphi$ means that $\varphi$ has a $D$-proof from
$\alpha$. $D$ is \emph{$\alpha$-sound} if $\alpha \vdash_D \varphi$ implies that
$\varphi$ holds in every model of $\alpha$, and \emph{$\alpha$-complete} if the
converse holds (2001, \S7, p.~37). All deduction methods in this essay
(Hilbert-style deduction, semantic tableaux, and Willard's tableau lists
below) are sound and complete in this sense. They differ in proof
\emph{length}, and self-justification is a phenomenon of length.

\begin{fact}[Willard 2001, Lemma~7.1B, p.~37, \texttt{full}]\label{fact:closure}
If $D$ is $\alpha$-sound and $\alpha$-complete, $\alpha \vdash_D \varphi$,
$\alpha \vdash_D \psi$, and $\varphi \wedge \psi \supset \chi$ holds in every
model of $\alpha$, then $\alpha \vdash_D \chi$.
\end{fact}

\noindent
So the theorems of $(\alpha, D)$ are closed under logical consequence. What
is not closed is the size of proofs: for a cut-free $D$, Willard notes, the
combined proof ``could then correspond to a bit string more than
exponentially larger than the combined lengths of the three initial proofs''
(2001, p.~38, text layer).

\paragraph{Languages.} Three languages occur.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item The language of arithmetic, with $0$, successor, $+$ and $\times$, and the
  usual classes $\Delta_0$, $\Pi_1$, $\Sigma_1$.
\item Willard's 2001 language: the seven \emph{grounding functions} (truncated
  subtraction, integer division, predecessor, maximum, logarithm, root, and
  $\mathit{Count}$, all \emph{non-growth}: $F(\vec a) \le \max(\vec a)$) and
  one constant symbol $\bar n$ for every natural number $n$ (2001, pp.~9--10).
  Its classes are written $\Delta_0^-$, $\Pi_1^-$, $\Sigma_1^-$. Addition is
  not a function symbol; Group-1 of $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ asserts its totality by the
  $\Pi_2^-$ axiom $\forall x\,\forall y\,\exists z\; z - x = y$ (2001, Eq.~6).
\item Willard's 2005 \emph{U-Grounding} language: six grounding functions
  plus the growth functions $+$ and $\mathit{Double}(x) = x + x$, eight
  function symbols in all, with constants $\bar c_0$, $\bar c_1$ for $0$ and
  $1$ (2005, \S2, p.~4, and \S3, p.~8). Every $n \ge 2$ is named by a term
  $\overline{n}$, Willard's ``U-Grounded Binary Representation'', with at
  most $2\log_2 n$ occurrences of $+$ and $\mathit{Double}$ applied to $1$
  (p.~4, where the overline is Willard's notation). Its classes are
  $\Delta_0^*$, $\Pi_1^*$, $\Sigma_1^*$; bounded quantifiers have the form
  $\forall v \le t$ and $\exists v \le t$ (p.~5). A negation written in front
  of unbounded quantifiers yields a sentence in neither class: for a proper
  $\Sigma_1^*$ sentence $\Upsilon$, ``$\neg\Upsilon$'' is ``technically viewed
  \ldots{} as not constituting a formal $\Pi_1^*$ sentence'', although it is
  logically equivalent to one (Remark~5, II, p.~27, text layer).
  Multiplication is not a function symbol; its graph
  $M(x,y,z)$ is a $\Delta_0^*$ formula (2005, Eq.~4), so the system knows
  multiplication's algebra but not its totality. Multiplication by any fixed
  constant is total, through iterated addition; what is withheld is the
  \emph{uniform} operation $x \cdot y$ (codified statement~\S3.1).
\end{itemize}
We write ``bounded'', ``$\Pi_1$-class'' and ``$\Sigma_1$-class'' for whichever
of these classes is in force. A bar over a number, $\bar n$, denotes the
constant for $n$ in the 2001 language and the term $\overline{n}$ in the 2005
language; no argument below needs a constant for every number.

\paragraph{Consistency notions.} For a configuration $(\alpha, D)$, Willard
distinguishes (2005, p.~5):
\emph{Level(0$-$)}: there is no $D$-proof of $0=1$ from $\alpha$;
\emph{Level(1)}: there is no $\Pi_1^*$ sentence $\Psi$ such that both $\Psi$ and
$\neg\Psi$ have $D$-proofs from $\alpha$. ``All the definitions of
consistency, from Level(0-) up to Level(n) for any $n$, are equivalent to
each other under strong enough models of arithmetic. However, many weak axiom
systems do not have a mathematical strength to recognize this equivalence''
(2005, p.~6, text layer). Willard's rule for choosing among them: ``it is
desirable to use the weakest possible definition \ldots{} when one is seeking
to generalize the Second Incompleteness Theorem. On the other hand, the
opposite is true when one seeks to develop `boundary-case exceptions' ''
(2002c, p.~2, text layer). This is why his negative results below use
Level(0$-$) and his positive ones Level(1).

\paragraph{Arithmetization.} For each configuration in this essay,
$\Prf^D_\alpha(x, y)$ (``$y$ is the Gödel number of a $D$-proof from $\alpha$
of the sentence with Gödel number $x$'') is a bounded formula, and
$\Prov^D_\alpha(x) := \exists y\, \Prf^D_\alpha(x,y)$. That this and the
other syntactic predicates below (substitution, $\Pair$, the Prenex$^*$
rewriting) have bounded definitions in Willard's languages is his
arithmetization of syntax. It runs through Wrathall's characterization of
the linear-time hierarchy by bounded formulas (2001, Theorems~C.1--C.2,
\texttt{sketch}; Theorem~C.3, \texttt{full} given them; 2005, p.~10). It is
the part of Gödel's work that Oberhoff packages as his Theorem~7, transposed
to languages without total multiplication. We take it as given, with that
status. Oberhoff's \fs{$\Prog{A}(\Prog{A})$ halts}, for the proof-searching
programs $\Prog{P}$, $\Prog{B}$, $\Prog{C}$ of this essay, is formalized as
``the search succeeds'': an existential statement about $\Prf$, not about
execution traces.

\paragraph{Encoding.} We fix the Gödel encoding. Proofs are encoded as in
2005, Appendix~A (pp.~39--40): a string of six-bit bytes read as an integer
in base~64, each of the 24 symbols coded between 32 and 55 (so with leading
bit~1), and a tableau list encoded as the concatenation of its components.
A sentence is numbered by the value of its own byte string, the convention
2001 states (Appendix~B, p.~51); 2005's Appendix~A encodes only proofs, and
we extend it in the same way. The first byte is the highest place, the
natural reading of ``an integer written in base 64''. Willard does not say
otherwise, and we fix it. Two consequences are used below. A string of $N$
bytes that begins with a symbol byte has value at least
$32 \cdot 64^{N-1}$; every proof and every sentence begins with one. And a
sentence that occurs in a proof has a Gödel number no larger than the
proof's, since a contiguous substring of a base-64 numeral has no larger
value. Every
axiom used in a tableau occurs in it as a node (p.~6: the nodes other than
the root ``are either axioms of $\alpha$ or deductions from higher nodes'').

\paragraph{Prenex form.} A semantic tableau proof of $\Phi$ is a closed tree
whose root ``stores the sentence $\neg\Phi$ (rewritten in Prenex$^*$ normal
form)'', a Prenex$^*$ sentence being a $\Pi_i^*$ or $\Sigma_i^*$ sentence
(2005, p.~6). So whether a tree proves $\Phi$ depends on $\Phi$ only through
that root. Willard does not fix the rewriting, and we fix it. For a
Prenex$^*$ sentence $\Theta = Q_1 v_1 \ldots Q_k v_k\, \delta$ with $\delta$
bounded, let $\mathrm{dual}(\Theta) := \bar Q_1 v_1 \ldots \bar Q_k v_k\,
\neg\delta$, with each quantifier exchanged for the other. Let
$\mathrm{pnx}(\Theta) := \Theta$, and $\mathrm{pnx}(\neg\Theta) :=
\mathrm{dual}(\mathrm{pnx}(\Theta))$. Willard's arguments use only that the
root is a Prenex$^*$ sentence equivalent to $\neg\Phi$, with the class and
validity level that sentence has (Remark~5, I, p.~27; the proof of
Theorem~2, pp.~23--26, and Appendix~B). So they hold under this convention,
and he adds that ``All our results will also hold if we drop the Prenex$^*$
requirement'' (p.~7, \texttt{stated-only}). The convention has the property
we need: for a Prenex$^*$ sentence $\Phi$,
\[
\mathrm{pnx}(\neg\neg\Phi) = \mathrm{dual}(\mathrm{dual}(\Phi)) =
\mathrm{pnx}(\neg\,\mathrm{dual}(\Phi)),
\]
so the syntactic negation $\neg\Phi$ and the Prenex$^*$ sentence
$\mathrm{dual}(\Phi)$ have exactly the same tableau proofs. They have the
same tableau-list proofs too, since a list proves the conclusion of its last
component (p.~7).

\paragraph{Pairing a sentence with its negation.} Willard's consistency
axiom uses a bounded formula $\Pair(x,y)$ for ``$x$ is the Gödel number of a
$\Pi_1^*$ sentence and $y$ is $x$'s negation'' (2005, p.~9), ``the sentence
that represents the negation of $x$'s formal statement'' (p.~34). His text
uses two candidates for $y$. Eq.~(28) writes $\Pair(\gn{f}, \gn{\neg f})$,
the string $\neg f$, which is in neither class (p.~5, and p.~26, footnote~3:
``neither $\Sigma_1^*$ nor $\Pi_1^*$ because a negation symbol appears to the
left of its unbounded quantifiers''). His proof of Theorem~1 uses ``the
$\Sigma_1^*$ theorem that is $\Phi$'s negation'' (p.~20), that is,
$\mathrm{dual}(\Phi)$. We fix $\Pair(x, y)$ to hold when $x$ codes a
$\Pi_1^*$ sentence $\Phi$ and $y$ codes a sentence $\theta$ with
$\mathrm{pnx}(\neg\theta) = \mathrm{pnx}(\neg\neg\Phi)$: a sentence whose
tableau proofs are exactly those of $\neg\Phi$. Both of Willard's candidates
qualify, and under the convention above they have the same proofs.

\paragraph{The Gödel sentence and the halting sentence.} Let $\Subst_0(g,h)$
be a bounded formula defining the graph of substitution: $h$ is the code of
the sentence obtained by substituting the numeral of $g$ into the formula
coded by $g$. Use it in least-witness form,
\[
\Subst(g,h) := \Subst_0(g,h) \wedge \forall h' \le h\,[h' = h \vee
\neg\Subst_0(g,h')],
\]
which is bounded and, in the standard model, holds exactly when $\Subst_0$
does. Let $n$ be the Gödel number of
\[
\Gamma(g) := \forall x\;\forall h\;\bigl[\Subst(g,h) \supset
\neg\Prf^D_\alpha(h,x)\bigr],
\]
and let $\G := \Gamma(\bar n)$ and $c := \gn{\G}$. $\G$ is a $\Pi_1$-class
sentence, and $\Subst(\bar n, \bar c)$ is a true bounded sentence. The
\emph{halting sentence} is
\[
\mathsf{H} := \mathrm{dual}(\G) = \exists x\,\exists h\,\neg\bigl[\Subst(\bar
n,h) \supset \neg\Prf^D_\alpha(h,x)\bigr],
\]
\fs{$\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ halts}: some $x$ is a proof of the sentence that
substitution produces. It is $\Sigma_1$-class, and $\Pair(\bar c, \bar c\,')$
is a true bounded sentence for $c' := \gn{\mathsf{H}}$.

Our $\G$ differs from Willard's fixed point (2001, Eq.~63, p.~38) in two
respects, both ours. It has no bound $h \le x$, as Willard's own bounded
Gödel sentence $\Mho$ has none (2005, Eq.~24, p.~33). And its substitution
formula is in least-witness form. Together these make the fixed point
provable from trichotomy alone, with no encoding hypothesis and no inherited
uniqueness theorem:

\begin{fact}[Fixed point; after Willard 2001, proof of Theorem~7.2, p.~39]
\label{fact:fp}
Let $D$ be $\alpha$-sound and $\alpha$-complete, let $\Prf^D_\alpha$ be
bounded, and let $\alpha$ prove the true bounded sentence
$\Subst(\bar n, \bar c)$. Then
\[
\alpha \vdash_D \G \supset \forall y\,\neg\Prf^D_\alpha(\bar c, y).
\tag{FP$\Rightarrow$}
\]
If $\alpha$ also proves trichotomy at $\bar c$,
$\forall h\,(h \le \bar c \vee \bar c \le h)$, then $\alpha$ proves the
uniqueness sentence $(U)\;\forall h\,[\Subst(\bar n, h) \supset h = \bar c]$
and
\[
\alpha \vdash_D \G \leftrightarrow \forall y\,\neg\Prf^D_\alpha(\bar c, y).
\tag{FP}
\]
\end{fact}

\begin{proof}
(FP$\Rightarrow$): $\G$ instantiated at $x = y$, $h = \bar c$, with
$\Subst(\bar n, \bar c)$, gives $\neg\Prf(\bar c, y)$. So
$\Subst(\bar n, \bar c)$ implies (FP$\Rightarrow$) in every structure.

(U): let $\Subst(\bar n, h)$. If $h \le \bar c$, the second conjunct of
$\Subst(\bar n, \bar c)$ at $h' = h$ gives $h = \bar c$ or
$\neg\Subst_0(\bar n, h)$, and the first conjunct of $\Subst(\bar n, h)$
excludes the latter. If $\bar c \le h$, the second conjunct of
$\Subst(\bar n, h)$ at $h' = \bar c$ gives $\bar c = h$ or
$\neg\Subst_0(\bar n, \bar c)$, and the first conjunct of
$\Subst(\bar n, \bar c)$ excludes the latter. So trichotomy and
$\Subst(\bar n, \bar c)$ imply (U) in every structure.

(FP$\Leftarrow$): given $x$ and $h$ with $\Subst(\bar n, h)$, (U) gives
$h = \bar c$, and the hypothesis gives $\neg\Prf(\bar c, x)$.

Each implication therefore follows in every model of $\alpha$ from theorems
of $\alpha$, and Fact~\ref{fact:closure}, applied repeatedly, gives its
$D$-proof.
\end{proof}

\noindent
$\mathsf{H}$ is logically equivalent to the syntactic negation $\neg\G$, and
Fact~\ref{fact:closure} gives
$\alpha \vdash_D \neg\G \leftrightarrow \mathsf{H}$, but that alone does not
give $\alpha \vdash_D \Prov(\gn{\neg\G}) \leftrightarrow
\Prov(\gn{\mathsf{H}})$: $\Prov$ is sensitive to codes, and such an
equivalence is a derivability condition, the very thing in question. What
our conventions give instead is that $\neg\G$ and $\mathsf{H}$ have the same
proofs in the standard model, and that $\Pair$ pairs $\G$ with both.

\paragraph{Relative consistency.} Every consistency result in this essay,
Willard's and ours, is a theorem of ordinary metamathematics proved under a
hypothesis, typically that the $\Pi_1$-class theorems of a base theory $A$
are true. That hypothesis is not decidable, and no system here is ``proved
consistent'' outright. How strong a metatheory these proofs need is a
separate question, which this essay does not address. An example is whether
Willard's consistency theorem is provable in elementary arithmetic from
uniform $\Pi_1$ reflection for $A$.

\section{Where the Faith Lives}
\label{sec:faith}

Here is the first half of Oberhoff's proof that $\G$ is undecidable (his \S4,
a second proof of his Theorem~4):

\Case{Suppose $\G$ is provable:}
\SubCase{Or $\neg\G$ isn't provable:}
\begin{steps}
\item Because $\G$ is provable $\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ will eventually find such
  a proof after some sequence of steps which we can write out in $\F$.
\item Because this sequence leads to a terminal state this gives a proof of
  $\fs{$\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ halts} = \neg\G$.\contra
\end{steps}

\smallskip\noindent
Theorem~6 asks $\F$ to run this argument on itself. Fix a configuration
$(\F, D)$ and write $\Prov$ for $\Prov^D_{\F}$. As statements that $\F$ must
prove, the argument has three steps.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*), leftmargin=2.5em]
\item $\mathsf{H} \supset \Prov(\bar c)$: \fs{if $\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ halts,
  then $\G$ is provable}. This is (FP$\Leftarrow$), contraposed. It is not
  literally trivial: $\mathsf{H}$ speaks of ``the sentence $h$ with
  $\Subst(\bar n, h)$'', and identifying $h$ with $\G$ needs the uniqueness
  sentence (U), which costs trichotomy (Fact~\ref{fact:fp}).
\item $\Prov(\bar c) \supset \Prov(\bar c\,')$, where $c' = \gn{\mathsf{H}}$:
  \fs{if $\G$ is provable, then \fs{$\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ halts} is provable}.
  This is the step ``$\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ will eventually find such a proof
  after some sequence of steps which we can write out in $\F$''. Inside $\F$
  it reads: \emph{for every proof $s$ of $\G$ there is a proof that
  $\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ halts}, namely the proof that exhibits $s$ and checks
  it.
\item $\Prov(\bar c) \wedge \Prov(\bar c\,') \supset \mathit{Inc}$, where
  $\mathit{Inc}$ is the negation of the consistency statement in use. For
  Level(1), whose statement is
  $\forall x\,\forall y\,\forall p\,\forall q\;\neg[\Pair(x,y) \wedge
  \Prf(x,p) \wedge \Prf(y,q)]$ with $\Pair$ as in \S\ref{sec:conventions}
  (2005, Eq.~6, p.~9), step (3) follows from this statement
  and the true bounded sentence $\Pair(\bar c, \bar c\,')$ by
  Fact~\ref{fact:closure}. For Level(0$-$) it is a formalized combination of
  two proofs into a proof of $0=1$, which for a cut-free $D$ is the expensive
  construction Fact~\ref{fact:closure} warns about.
\end{enumerate}
Chaining (1)--(3) gives $\F \vdash_D \mathsf{H} \supset \mathit{Inc}$, that is,
$\F \vdash_D \neg\mathit{Inc} \supset \G$: \fs{$\F$ is consistent
$\Rightarrow \G$}, which is what Theorem~6 needs.

The only step that asks $\F$ to know something substantial about itself is
(2). In the first incompleteness theorem, ``which we can write out in $\F$''
was a fact \emph{about} $\F$, established by us from outside: we are handed a
halting run and we produce the proof. In the second theorem it has to be a
theorem \emph{of} $\F$, stated for every $s$ at once. The two deserve
separate names.

\begin{definition}[Watching]\label{def:watch}
$(\F, D)$ \emph{watches} if $\F \vdash_D \sigma$ for every true
$\Sigma_1$-class sentence $\sigma$ of its language.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Knowing that one watches]\label{def:know}
$(\F, D)$ \emph{knows that it watches} at a sentence $\sigma$ if
$\F \vdash_D \sigma \supset \Prov(\gn{\sigma})$. It \emph{knows that it
watches} if it does so at every $\Sigma_1$-class sentence.
\end{definition}

\noindent
A program halts if and only if a $\Sigma_1$ sentence about it is true, so
watching is external $\Sigma_1$-completeness, and knowing that one watches is
formalized $\Sigma_1$-completeness, sentence by sentence. Section~\ref{sec:transcripts}
adds its uniform version, with a free variable. The third Hilbert--Bernays--Löb
derivability condition~\cite{hb39,lob55}, $\Prov(\gn{\varphi}) \supset
\Prov(\gn{\Prov(\gn{\varphi})})$, is the instance at the $\Sigma_1$ sentences
$\sigma = \Prov(\gn{\varphi})$, the halting statements of proof searches.

\begin{lemma}[ours]\label{lem:instances}
Let $(\F, D)$ watch, with $D$ $\F$-sound and $\F$-complete and $\Prf^D_\F$
correct in the standard model (it holds of $(h, x)$ exactly when $x$ codes a
$D$-proof from $\F$ of the sentence coded $h$).
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $(\F, D)$ knows that it watches at every \emph{true} $\Sigma_1$-class
  sentence.
\item If (FP) holds, step (2) is $\F$-provably equivalent to knowing that one
  watches at $\mathsf{H}$: $\F \vdash_D (2) \leftrightarrow [\mathsf{H}
  \supset \Prov(\bar c\,')]$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) Let $\sigma$ be true. Then $\F \vdash_D \sigma$ by watching, so a
$D$-proof of $\sigma$ exists, $\Prov(\gn{\sigma})$ is a true $\Sigma_1$-class
sentence, and
watching gives $\F \vdash_D \Prov(\gn{\sigma})$. Since
$\Prov(\gn{\sigma}) \supset (\sigma \supset \Prov(\gn{\sigma}))$ is valid,
Fact~\ref{fact:closure} gives the implication. (b) (FP) and
$\mathsf{H} \leftrightarrow \neg\G$ are theorems of $\F$, so by soundness
every model of $\F$ satisfies $\mathsf{H} \leftrightarrow \Prov(\bar c)$. The
two implications differ only by
this replacement in the antecedent, so their equivalence holds in every model
of $\F$, and Fact~\ref{fact:closure} gives the $D$-proof.
\end{proof}

\noindent
So the faith step is not a claim about runs that happen. Those a watching
system sees, and Lemma~\ref{lem:instances}(a) gives it the knowledge for
free. The faith is a claim about a run that does not happen: if $\G$ is
true, $\mathsf{H}$ is false, and step (2) asserts that
\emph{had} $\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ halted, $\F$ would have seen it.

Here is what each result of~\cite{oberhoff} uses:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llccc@{}}
\toprule
Result in~\cite{oberhoff} & Assumes about $\F$ & Watches & Knows it watches \\
\midrule
Thm 4 (G1 via halting) & honest; complete (for contradiction) & --- & --- \\
\S4, $\G$ undecidable & consistent; honest in case 2 & case 1 & --- \\
Thm 5 (Rosser, via $\Prog{B}$) & consistent & both cases & --- \\
Thm 6 (G2) & consistent & yes & step (2) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
Theorem 4 needs only that $\F$ can \emph{state} halting facts honestly; the
search does the rest. Case 2 of the \S4 argument uses only that $\F$'s
$\Sigma_1$ theorems are true. Rosser's case 2 also uses a $\Pi_1$ fact, that
a run which has entered \texttt{while true \{\}} never leaves it; Oberhoff's
footnote~12: ``Any formal system capable of reasoning about algorithms can
prove that `while true \{\}' runs forever; otherwise it ought to be ashamed
of itself.'' Apart from that, every result in the essay except the second
incompleteness theorem uses watching and nothing more. The classical proof of
the second theorem runs through the derivability conditions; the
computational retelling shows what the third condition \emph{says}.

\section{Transcripts Are Bigger Than Runs}
\label{sec:transcripts}

Why should knowing be harder than watching? The natural proof of step (2)
takes a proof $s$ of $\G$ and produces the proof $t$ of
\fs{$\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ halts} that exhibits $s$ and checks it. In a language
whose only growth functions are $+$ and $\mathit{Double}$, exhibiting $s$
costs a term, and terms are expensive.

\begin{lemma}[ours]\label{lem:transcript}
In Willard's 2005 language and encoding, every semantic tableau proof that
contains a closed term of value $v \ge 2$ has Gödel number greater than
$v^5$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A closed term with $L$ function symbols built from $\bar c_0$, $\bar c_1$ has
value at most $2^L$: non-growth functions do not increase the maximum of their
arguments, and $+$ and $\mathit{Double}$ at most double it. This is the
doubling estimate of case (c) in Willard's proof of Lemma~1 (2005, p.~24),
applied to a term with no parameters. So a term of value $v$ has
$L \ge \log_2 v$ function symbols. The U-Length of a node is the number of
U-Grounding function symbols in its sentence; its U-Depth is its own
U-Length plus the U-Lengths of all its ancestors; and the U-Height of the
tree is the largest U-Depth of a leaf (p.~22). So the node containing the
term has U-Depth at least $L$. If that node is a leaf, the U-Height is at
least its U-Depth. If not, a leaf below it has a U-Depth at least as large,
since the nodes in between add non-negative U-Lengths. Either way the tree
has U-Height at least $L$. Willard's Eq.~(20) (p.~26) is $\text{U-Height}(p) <
\tfrac15 \log_2 p$, which, he says, Appendix~A's encoding ``as well as any
other reasonable encoding methodology, will clearly satisfy'' (text layer).
Hence $\log_2 p > 5L \ge 5\log_2 v$.
\end{proof}

\noindent
So the transcript $t$ of step (2) is bigger than the fifth power of the run
$s$ it records, and the natural proof of step (2) asks $\F$ to believe that
$s \mapsto t$ is defined everywhere. Willard's systems cannot believe that of
any function that outgrows every linear one. The reason lies in how a proof
from universal axioms produces the numbers it claims exist.

\begin{proposition}[Linear witnesses; ours]\label{prop:herbrand}
Let $\F$ be a set of sentences of Willard's 2005 language, each bounded or
$\Pi_1^*$-class, all true in the standard model, and let
$\delta(\vec x, \vec y)$ be bounded.
If $\F \vdash \forall \vec x\, \exists \vec y\, \delta(\vec x, \vec y)$ (in
first-order logic, hence by any sound deduction method), then there is a
natural number $k$ such that for all $\vec x$ some
$\vec y \le k\,(x_1 + \cdots + x_m + 1)$ satisfies $\delta$. The same holds in
Willard's 2001 language for a set of true sentences, each bounded or
$\Pi_1^-$-class, together with the axiom that addition is total.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\emph{The 2005 case.} Write $|\vec x|$ for $x_1 + \cdots + x_m + 1$, and
$\bar k \cdot t$ for the term $t + \cdots + t$ with $k$ summands. Suppose that
no $k$ works. Then for each $k$ some $\vec x$ has no witness
$\vec y \le k |\vec x|$. So each finite subset of $\mathrm{Th}(\mathbb N)
\cup \{\forall \vec y \le \bar k \cdot |\vec c|\; \neg\delta(\vec c, \vec y) :
k \in \mathbb N\}$, with new constants $\vec c$, is satisfied in $\mathbb N$
by interpreting $\vec c$ as such an $\vec x$ for the largest $k$ involved. By
compactness there is a model $M$ of $\mathrm{Th}(\mathbb N)$ with elements
$\vec a$ such that $M \models \forall \vec y \le \bar k \cdot |\vec a|\;
\neg\delta(\vec a, \vec y)$ for every $k \in \mathbb N$. Let
\[
I := \{\, z \in M : z \le \bar k \cdot |\vec a| \text{ for some } k \in
\mathbb N \,\}.
\]
The following facts hold because the sentences expressing them are true in
$\mathbb N$, hence in $M$: $\le$ is transitive, so $I$ is an initial segment;
$\bar n \le \bar n \cdot u$ for $u \ge 1$, so $I$ contains every $\bar n$;
and $x \le \bar i \cdot u$, $y \le \bar j \cdot u$ imply
$x + y \le \overline{i + j} \cdot u$ and $\mathit{Double}(x) \le
\overline{2i} \cdot u$, so $I$ is closed under $+$ and $\mathit{Double}$.
The six non-growth functions satisfy $F(\vec z) \le \max(\vec z)$ (2005,
p.~4, as a true sentence of each), so $I$ is closed under them too. Hence $I$
is a substructure of $M$ and an initial segment. A bounded formula therefore
has the same truth value in $I$ as in $M$ at arguments from $I$: its terms
take the same values, and its quantifiers range over initial segments of
$I$. A bounded sentence of $\F$ is true in $M$, hence in $I$. A
$\Pi_1^*$-class sentence $\forall \vec z\, \beta(\vec z)$ of $\F$ is true in
$M$, so $\beta$ holds at every tuple from $I$, in $M$ and hence in $I$. So $I
\models \F$, hence $I \models \forall \vec x\, \exists \vec y\, \delta$, and
some $\vec y \in I$ satisfies $\delta(\vec a, \vec y)$ in $I$, hence in $M$.
But $\vec y \le \bar k \cdot |\vec a|$ for some $k \in \mathbb N$, against the
choice of $\vec a$.

\emph{The 2001 case.} Addition is not a function symbol there, but its graph
$z \mathbin{\dot-} x = y \wedge x \le z$ is a bounded formula, and the true
sentences that it is total (2001, Eq.~6) and single-valued hold in every
model of $\mathrm{Th}(\mathbb N)$. So expand $\mathbb N$ and $M$ by the
function it defines, and write the bounds $\bar k \cdot |\vec x|$ with it.
Each such bound is expressible by a bounded formula through the graph. The
argument above then runs verbatim: $I$ is closed under the defined addition
and under the grounding functions, which are non-growth; it contains every
constant $\bar n$; and the bounded and $\Pi_1^-$-class sentences of $\F$ hold
in $I$. The axiom that addition is total holds in $I$, because for
$x, y \in I$ the element $z = x + y$ lies in $I$ and satisfies
$z \mathbin{\dot-} x = y$.\contra
\end{proof}

\noindent
The constant $k$ can be large. For intuition, though the proof above does
not need it: Herbrand's theorem would present the witnesses as terms, and a
term of depth $d$ built from $+$, $\mathit{Double}$ and non-growth functions
is at most $2^d$ times its largest argument. So $k$ is like $2^d$:
exponential in the proof, but fixed once the proof is. The input $\vec x$
ranges over all numbers, so the witnesses grow linearly in the input, with
the proof's size in the constant. Willard's own engine makes the same
comparison from the other side: no proof can name a number much larger than
its own Gödel number. With multiplication as a total function, a term of
depth $d$ can grow like $x^{2^d}$ in its input and name numbers like
$2^{2^d}$. That is the reason Willard's construction fails there
(\S\ref{sec:price}).

Write $\mathrm{Num}(x, y)$ for a bounded formula saying that $y$ is the Gödel
number of the sentence $\bar x = \bar x$. For a bounded formula $\delta(x)$,
say that $(\F, D)$ \emph{knows uniformly that it watches} $\delta$ if $\F
\vdash_D \forall x\, [\delta(x) \supset \exists y\, \exists q\,
(\mathrm{Num}_\delta(x, y) \wedge \Prf(y, q))]$, where
$\mathrm{Num}_\delta(x,y)$ says that $y$ codes $\delta(\bar x)$. This is
formalized $\Sigma_1$-completeness with a free variable. Recursively
axiomatized extensions of $I\Sigma_1$ prove it for their canonical
provability predicate, and derive the third derivability condition from
it~\cite{hp93}.

\begin{corollary}[ours]\label{cor:uniform}
Under the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:willard}(a), with $\Pair$ as fixed
in \S\ref{sec:conventions}, $\ISD$ does not prove
$\forall x\, \exists y\; y = x \cdot x$ (with multiplication's bounded graph
$M$), nor $\forall x\, \exists y\, \mathrm{Num}(x, y)$, ``every number has a
written name''. So it does not know uniformly that it watches even the
formula $x = x$. Under the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:willard}(b), the
same holds for $\mathrm{IS}(A)$. The deduction method enters only through
the truth of Group-3: since the theorems of any sound and complete method are
the first-order consequences of the axioms (Fact~\ref{fact:closure}), the
unprovability holds for every such method once the axioms are true.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Every axiom of $\ISD$ is a true sentence, bounded or $\Pi_1^*$-class. For
Groups 0--2 this follows from the hypothesis and Willard's remark that they
``can be encoded as $\Pi_1^*$ sentences'' (2005, p.~20); Group-3 is true by
Theorem~\ref{thm:willard}(a) (Lemma~\ref{lem:honest} below). Every axiom of
$\mathrm{IS}(A)$ is true by Lemma~\ref{lem:honest}(b). Apart from the
totality of addition, each is bounded (the Group-0 atoms, 2001, p.~10) or
$\Pi_1^-$-class (Group-1's other axioms, Group-2, and Group-3 by Theorem~C.3).
So
Proposition~\ref{prop:herbrand} applies. The witness $y = x^2$ exceeds
$k(x+1)$ once $x > 2k$. The only $y$ with $\mathrm{Num}(x, y)$ is the code of
$\bar x = \bar x$. In 2005, $\bar x$ is a term with at least $\log_2 x$
function symbols (as in Lemma~\ref{lem:transcript}), one byte each, so by the
encoding of \S\ref{sec:conventions} the code is at least
$32 \cdot 64^{\log_2 x - 1} = x^6/2$. In 2001, $\bar x$ is a constant of
$\lceil \log_{32}(x+1)\rceil + 1$ bytes (Appendix~B, p.~51), and the code is
at least $32\,(x+1)^{6/5}$. Either bound eventually exceeds $k(x+1)$. The
uniform statement for $x = x$ implies $\forall x\, \exists y\,
\mathrm{Num}(x, y)$.
\end{proof}

\noindent
So the faith step fails in Willard's systems in its uniform form, for the
plainest reason: they cannot prove that numbers can be written down, because
writing a number down costs more than linearly many bits. Each particular
number they can name and each particular run they can check; that is
watching. The faith step in Oberhoff's proof, step (2), is one closed sentence
about the Gödel sentence, though, and there the totality claim is vacuous. If
$\G$ is true, no proof $s$ of $\G$ exists, so ``for every $s$ there is a
transcript'' holds trivially, and Proposition~\ref{prop:herbrand} yields no
contradiction. Section~\ref{sec:backwards} proves step (2) unprovable by a
different route, and Section~\ref{sec:bought} shows a bounded transcript
statement at work in a theorem of Willard's.

Willard's first statement of the idea continues this essay's epigraph (TR
93-10, p.~7, verbatim):
\begin{quote}\small
This means there will always be adequate memory for writing $b_n$'s binary
encoding when there is sufficient memory for storing a semantic tableaux
proof of its existence (whereas the same is plainly not true \emph{when
multiplication is assumed to be a function}).

For instance, multiplication allows one to establish the existence of a
number $a_{300}$ (in a proof with approximately 300 lines), whose binary
encoding requires \emph{more digits} than the number of atoms in the
universe.
\end{quote}
Here $b_{i+1} = b_i + b_i$ and $a_{i+1} = (a_i)^2$. Starting both at~2,
$b_n = 2^{n+1}$ and $a_n = 2^{2^n}$: a proof of $n$ doublings names a number
of $n+1$ bits, and a proof of $n$ squarings names one of $2^n$ bits. Willard's
later statement compares the Gödel number of a proof with the integer it
shows to exist (2006a, Remark~3, p.~24, verbatim):
\begin{quote}\small
In particular, every proof of the existence of an integer $N$ using the
additive convention will have a larger Gödel number than $N$. In contrast,
the multiplicative convention allows for the Gödel number of such proofs to
have a sharply smaller magnitude than $N$ (when for instance
$N = 2^{2^k}$ and $k$ is larger than some fixed constant). In essence, this
difference in magnitude is the reason the Second Incompleteness Theorem will
generalize under the multiplicative naming paradigm --- while Section 4
showed boundary-case exceptions to it exist under the additive naming
convention.
\end{quote}

\section{A Machine That Says ``I Am Consistent''}
\label{sec:genius}

Oberhoff's moral for the second theorem is ``if you declare yourself a very
stable genius, you're not''. Let us build the genius with his tools. Fix a
base system $\Fo$, a deduction method $D$, and a consistency level, and
consider

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}
C(A) {
  for s $\in$ all possible strings {
    if s is a contradiction in $\Fo + \fs{A(A) doesn't halt}$ {
      halt;
    }
  }
}
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}
where a \emph{contradiction} is a $D$-proof of $0=1$ (Level(0$-$)) or a pair
of $D$-proofs of a $\Pi_1$-class sentence and of its syntactic negation
(Level(1)).
(We picked $\Prog{C}$ because it is a circle that never quite closes;
\S\ref{sec:machine} explains the joke.) Let
\[
\W = \fs{$\Prog{C}(\Prog{C})$ doesn't halt}, \qquad \F = \Fo + \W .
\]
Just as $\G$ says ``I am not provable'', $\W$ says that $\F$ is consistent at
the chosen level. So $\F$ proves its own consistency in one line: the claim is
an axiom.

$\Prog{C}$ is not an analogy. It is Willard's construction read as a program.
In 2005 (pp.~9--10), $\mathit{ExPrf}^D_{\mathrm{UNION}(A)}(h,t,p)$ says that
$p$ is a $D$-proof of $t$ from Groups 0--2 plus the sentence coded $h$, and
$\mathit{SubstPrf}^D_{\mathrm{UNION}(A)}(g,t,p)$, his Eq.~(7), combines it
with $\Subst(g,h)$: the input $\Prog{A}$ is $g$, and \fs{$\Prog{A}(\Prog{A})$
doesn't halt} is the sentence $h$ with $\Subst(g,h)$. His Group-3 axiom,
Eq.~(8), is $\Gamma_3(\bar n_3)$, where $n_3$ is the Gödel number of
\[
\Gamma_3(g) := \forall x\,\forall y\,\forall p\,\forall q\;\neg\bigl[\Pair(x,y)
\wedge \mathit{SubstPrf}^D_{\mathrm{UNION}(A)}(g,x,p) \wedge
\mathit{SubstPrf}^D_{\mathrm{UNION}(A)}(g,y,q)\bigr]
\]
(Willard writes $\Gamma$ and $n$; we add the subscript to keep it apart from
the Gödel sentence's $\Gamma$ and $n$ of \S\ref{sec:conventions}). This is
\fs{$\Prog{C}(\Prog{C})$ doesn't halt} at Level(1); 2001's
Appendix~B does the same at Level(0$-$). Willard (2016, Definition~3.4 and
Example~3.5) remarks that constructing such a sentence is ``relatively easy,
via an application of the Fixed Point Theorem, but this sentence is
ironically typically useless!''

\begin{proposition}[The genius falls; standard]\label{prop:genius}
Let $\Fo$ be a recursively axiomatized extension of $I\Sigma_1$ in the
language of arithmetic, $D$ Hilbert-style deduction, $\Prov$ the canonical
$\Sigma_1$ provability predicate, and $\W$ as above at either level. Then
$\F = \Fo + \W$ is inconsistent, and $\Prog{C}(\Prog{C})$ halts.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $\F$ is consistent.
\begin{topsteps}
\item $\F$ proves its own consistency: $\W$ is one of its axioms, and by the
  diagonal lemma $I\Sigma_1$ proves $\W$ equivalent to the consistency
  statement of $\Fo + \W$ at the chosen level, for the canonical proof
  predicate. $I\Sigma_1$ also proves the two levels equivalent for $\F$: it
  proves explosion for the canonical Hilbert proof predicate, so a proof of
  $0=1$ yields proofs of every sentence, and it proves that proofs of a
  sentence and of its negation combine, by modus ponens, into a proof of
  $0=1$.
\item $\F$ is a recursively axiomatized extension of $I\Sigma_1$, so the
  derivability conditions hold for its canonical provability predicate,
  provably in $I\Sigma_1$~\cite{hp93}. In Oberhoff's terms, $\F$ knows that it
  watches, and steps (1)--(3) are theorems of $\F$.
\item So by the second incompleteness theorem (Oberhoff's Theorem~6, with
  its faith step now a theorem), $\F$ cannot prove its own
  consistency.\contra
\end{topsteps}
So $\F$ is inconsistent. A contradiction from $\F$ is then a finite object
that exists, and $\Prog{C}(\Prog{C})$ finds it.
\end{proof}

Willard records that ``Kleene, Rogers and Jeroslow [\ldots] each warned
their readers that most axiom systems, similar to $\alpha^*$, were useless on
account of their inconsistency, although they were technically
well-defined'' (2005, \S1). Two of his own theorems are instances in his own
languages. $\mathrm{XIS}^\lambda(\mathrm{PAX})$, his tableau system with
multiplication total and a reflection-style Group-3 axiom, ``is inconsistent''
for every $\lambda$ (2001, Theorem~7.3, p.~40, \texttt{full}). And
Section~\ref{sec:bought} describes the finest instance, where the system
falls because of one added axiom saying that one number exists.

\section{Willard's Machine}
\label{sec:machine}

Willard keeps $\Prog{C}$ and $\W$ and changes $\Fo$ and $D$. We describe the
2005 system $\ISD$ and note where the 2001 system $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ differs.

\paragraph{The system $\ISD$ (2005, \S3, pp.~8--10).} $A$ is any axiom system
in the U-Grounding language whose proof predicate $\Prf_A$ is $\Delta_0^*$, and
$D$ is a deduction method.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \textbf{Group-0} (names): four axioms fixing $\bar c_0$, $\bar c_1$, $+$
  and $\mathit{Double}$, so that every $n \ge 2$ has the name
  $\overline{n}$ of $O(\log n)$ symbols.
\item \textbf{Group-1} (watching): a finite set of true $\Pi_1^*$ sentences
  which, with Group-0, proves every $\Delta_0^*$ sentence true in the
  standard model. Willard's Table~I (2001, pp.~63--64) is one such set.
\item \textbf{Group-2} (inheritance): for each $\Pi_1^*$ sentence $\Phi$, the
  axiom $\forall p\,\{\Prf_A(\gn{\Phi}, p) \to \Phi\}$ (2005, Eq.~5). The
  schema is indexed by all $\Pi_1^*$ sentences, not by $A$'s theorems; for
  $\Phi$ that $A$ does not prove, the axiom is vacuously true.
\item \textbf{Group-3} (self-reference): the single $\Pi_1^*$ sentence
  $\Gamma_3(\bar n_3)$ of \S\ref{sec:genius}, Level(1) consistency of $\ISD$
  under $D$, with $\Prf^D_{\ISD}(t,p)$ encoded as
  $\mathit{SubstPrf}^D_{\mathrm{UNION}(A)}(\bar n_3, t, p)$ (p.~10). Willard
  stresses that the $\Pi_1^*$ form is essential (2005, p.~10).
\end{itemize}
The deduction methods are \emph{semantic tableaux} (2005, \S2, pp.~6--7: a
tree rooted at $\neg\Phi$ in prenex form, with elimination rules only and no
cut, every branch closed by some $\Upsilon$ and $\neg\Upsilon$) and
\emph{$\mathrm{Tab}{-}U_1^*{-}\mathrm{List}$}: a list of tableau proofs, each
of which may use the earlier conclusions as axioms, all intermediate
conclusions being $\Pi_1^*$ or $\Sigma_1^*$ (2005, p.~7). The latter is a
restricted modus ponens: lemmas are allowed, provided each has at most one
unbounded quantifier block.

$\mathrm{IS}(A)$ of 2001 differs in three respects: its language is the 2001
grounding language, with a constant for every number and addition total by
an axiom rather than a function symbol; $D$ is semantic tableaux; and its
Group-3 axiom asserts Level(0$-$) consistency,
$\forall y\,\neg\mathit{SemPrf}_{\mathrm{IS}(A)}(\gn{0=1}, y)$ (2001,
Eq.~8), with a $\Pi_1^-$ encoding (2001, Theorem~C.3, p.~60). Willard assumes
throughout that $A$ extends Peano Arithmetic (2001, p.~9).

\begin{theorem}[Willard]\label{thm:willard}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item (2005, Theorems~3 and~5, pp.~27, 29, \texttt{full}.) If every
  $\Pi_1^*$ theorem of $A$ is true in the standard model, then $\ISD$ is
  consistent, both for $D$ = semantic tableaux and for $D$ =
  $\mathrm{Tab}{-}U_1^*{-}\mathrm{List}$. The proof (via Theorem~1) rules out
  $D$-proofs of a $\Pi_1^*$ sentence $\Phi$ and of ``the $\Sigma_1^*$ theorem
  that is $\Phi$'s negation'' (p.~20), $\mathrm{dual}(\Phi)$. With $\Pair$ as
  fixed in \S\ref{sec:conventions}, a proof of any $\theta$ with
  $\Pair(\gn{\Phi}, \gn{\theta})$ is a proof of $\mathrm{dual}(\Phi)$, so
  Group-3 is true: Level(1) consistency.
\item (2001, Theorem~4.3, p.~20, \texttt{full}.) If $A$ is \emph{regularly
  consistent}, meaning that $\Prf_A$ has a $\Delta_0^-$ encoding and every
  Group-2 axiom of $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ is true (2001, p.~12), then
  $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ is consistent: there is no tableau proof of $0=1$, which
  is Level(0$-$).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Both are relative consistency theorems in the sense of
\S\ref{sec:conventions}. Willard also states that IS's construction fails for
Hilbert deduction: by Pudlák and Solovay, ``if $D$ denotes Hilbert deduction
then our $\mathrm{IS}_D(A)$ system will be automatically inconsistent'' (2005,
p.~8, text layer).

\begin{lemma}[ours]\label{lem:watch}
For $D$ = semantic tableaux or $\mathrm{Tab}{-}U_1^*{-}\mathrm{List}$,
$(\ISD, D)$ watches.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $\sigma = \exists \vec x\,\delta(\vec x)$ be a true $\Sigma_1^*$ sentence
with witnesses $\vec k$. Then $\delta(\overline{\vec k})$ is a true
$\Delta_0^*$ sentence, which Groups 0 and 1 prove, and $\sigma$ is its
logical consequence. Apply Fact~\ref{fact:closure}.
\end{proof}

\noindent
The same holds for $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ with $A \supseteq \mathrm{PAX}$: a system
that verifies the $\Pi_1^-$ theorems of $\mathrm{PAX}$ ``obviously has a
capacity to prove every $\Delta_0^-$ sentence which is true'' (2001, p.~39,
text layer), and $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ names every number by a constant. So a true
$\Sigma_1^-$ sentence $\exists \vec x\, \delta(\vec x)$, with witnesses $\vec
k$, has the true $\Delta_0^-$ instance $\delta(\bar{\vec k})$, which
$\mathrm{IS}(A)$ proves, and Fact~\ref{fact:closure} adds the existential
quantifiers, as in Lemma~\ref{lem:watch}. For general programs, Willard notes that the predicate
$\mathit{Turing}(x,y,z)$ is expressible in $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ (TR 93-10, p.~6),
so halting of any program is a $\Sigma_1^-$ sentence there; the arguments
below need only the proof-searchers, whose halting is formalized through
$\Prf$ directly.

\begin{lemma}[ours]\label{lem:honest}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If every $\Pi_1^*$ theorem of $A$ is true, then every theorem of $\ISD$
  is true in the standard model, and its proof predicate
  $\mathit{SubstPrf}^D_{\mathrm{UNION}(A)}(\bar n_3, \cdot, \cdot)$ is correct
  there.
\item If $A$ is regularly consistent, then every theorem of $\mathrm{IS}(A)$
  is true in the standard model.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) \emph{Correctness.} By Willard's arithmetization (\S\ref{sec:conventions}),
$\Subst$, $\Prf^D_{\mathrm{UNION}(A)}$ and $\mathit{ExPrf}^D_{\mathrm{UNION}(A)}$
are correct bounded definitions. A $D$-proof $p$ from $\ISD$ either uses
Group-3 or not. If not, it satisfies the first disjunct of Eq.~(7). If it
does, Group-3 occurs in it as a node, so by the encoding of
\S\ref{sec:conventions} Group-3's code $h_3$ satisfies $h_3 \le p$, and the
second disjunct holds with $h = h_3$. Conversely each disjunct describes a
$D$-proof from $\ISD$, given that $\Subst(\bar n_3, h)$ holds only for
$h = h_3$.

\emph{Truth.} Groups 0 and 1 are true (Willard: ``It is obvious that all
$\mathrm{IS}_D(A)$'s Group-0 and 1 axioms are valid under the standard
model'', 2005, p.~20, text layer). Group-2 is true by hypothesis. Group-3 is
true by Theorem~\ref{thm:willard}(a), as noted there. Theorems of true axioms
under a sound $D$ are true.

(b) \emph{Correctness.} $\mathrm{IS}(A)$'s Group-3 axiom is $\Theta(\bar N)$,
with $\Theta(g) := \forall y\, \neg\mathit{SubstSemPrf}_{\mathrm{UNION}(A)}(\gn{0=1},
y, g)$, where $\mathit{SubstSemPrf}_{\mathrm{UNION}(A)}(s, y, g)$ says that $y$ is
a tableau proof of $s$ from $\mathrm{UNION}(A)$ together with the sentence
$h$ such that $\Subst(g, h)$ (2001, Eqs.~86--87, p.~52). Its $\Delta_0^-$
definition is Willard's: item~25 of Theorem~C.1 and Theorem~C.2 (both
\texttt{sketch}). His test examines each node $s$ of $y$ for being the
Group-3 axiom, searching for $h$ with $\Subst(g,h)$ under the bound
$h \le s$: ``We may assume that our encoding scheme is designed so that
$h \le s$ when $h$ exists'' (Appendix~C, item~24 and p.~59, text layer). In
2001's encoding each tableau node opens with a curly bracket followed by its
sentence (Appendix~B, p.~52). So a node's byte string is a contiguous part
of $y$, $s \le y$, and the bounds cover every proof that uses the axiom, by
the node argument of~(a). Hence the formula holds in the
standard model exactly of the tableau proofs from $\mathrm{IS}(A)$, and the
proof predicate $\mathit{SubstSemPrf}_{\mathrm{UNION}(A)}(\cdot, \cdot,
\bar N)$ of $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ is correct.

\emph{Truth.} Group-0 consists of true atomic facts about the constants
(2001, p.~10). Group-1 is true. Group-2 is true by regular consistency
(2001, p.~12). Group-3, which by correctness says that no tableau proof of
$0=1$ from $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ exists, is true by
Theorem~\ref{thm:willard}(b). Tableaux are sound.
\end{proof}

\noindent
So $\ISD$ and $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ are honest in Oberhoff's sense, under the
hypotheses of Lemma~\ref{lem:honest}. If in addition $A$ proves the
$\Pi_1^*$ theorems of $\mathrm{PA}{+}$ (Peano Arithmetic with the U-Grounding
function symbols, 2005, p.~32), $\ISD$ obeys the first incompleteness
theorem. By Oberhoff's \S4 argument, spelled out as
Proposition~\ref{prop:backwards}(b) and Corollary~\ref{cor:isd}(a) below,
$\G$ is unprovable; $\neg\G$ and $\mathsf{H}$ are false, hence unprovable by
honesty. Rosser's version also goes through. The same holds for
$\mathrm{IS}(A)$ with $A \supseteq \mathrm{PAX}$ regularly consistent: the
proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:backwards}(b) needs only Level(0$-$)
consistency, watching and $\Subst(\bar n, \bar c)$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:is}
below), and honesty is Lemma~\ref{lem:honest}(b). In both
cases the finite search that Oberhoff writes out is a true bounded fact,
which Groups 0 and 1 prove. In case 2, $\Prog{B}(\Prog{B})$ enters its loop
at a definite step, and $\mathrm{PA}{+}$ proves the $\Pi_1^*$ sentence $\Phi$
= \fs{$\Prog{B}(\Prog{B})$ doesn't halt}. Group-2 hands it to $\ISD$ by
detachment: $A$ proves $\Phi$ with some proof $p$, Groups 0 and 1 prove the
true $\Delta_0^*$ sentence $\Prf_A(\gn{\Phi}, \bar p)$, and with $\Phi$'s
Group-2 axiom Fact~\ref{fact:closure} gives $\Phi$. For $\mathrm{IS}(A)$
the loop sentence is the corresponding $\Pi_1^-$ sentence of the 2001
language, which $\mathrm{PAX}$ proves; the Group-2 axiom of 2001, Eq.~(7)
(p.~10), hands it over by the same detachment. Willard
said so in his first technical report: $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ is ``a class of structures, lying halfway between
Peano Arithmetic and Presburger Arithmetic, which satisfy Godel's First but
not Second Incompleteness Theorem'' (TR 93-10, p.~6, verbatim).

\paragraph{Why it is consistent, in Oberhoff's format.} Willard's 2005 proof
needs two definitions. A $\Pi_1^*$ sentence $\forall\vec x\,\psi$ is
\emph{App$\forall(a)$-valid} if $\psi$ holds for all $\vec x \le a$; a
$\Sigma_1^*$ sentence $\exists\vec x\,\psi$ is \emph{App$\exists(b)$-valid} if
it has a witness $\vec x \le b$ (Eqs.~16--17; bounded quantifiers are left
alone). For $a > b \ge 1$, $\alpha$ is \emph{Normed$(a,b)$} if every axiom is a
$\Pi_1^*$ sentence with App$\forall(a)$ validity or a $\Sigma_1^*$ sentence
with App$\exists(b)$ validity, in the U-Grounding language with no other
function symbol (Definition~4, p.~19). $D$ is
\emph{$\theta$-compactified}, for a constant $0 < \theta < 1/3$, if whenever
$p \le \lceil a/b\rceil + 1$ is a $D$-proof of $\Phi$ from a Normed$(a,b)$
system, a $\Sigma_1^*$ $\Phi$ is App$\exists(b \cdot p^\theta)$-valid and a
$\Pi_1^*$ $\Phi$ is App$\forall(a / p^\theta)$-valid (Definition~5, p.~19). In
words: a proof $p$ can push an existential witness out by at most a factor
$p^\theta$, and can shrink a universal's certified range by at most the same
factor. Tableaux are $\tfrac14$-compactified (Theorem~2, p.~26,
\texttt{full}), and so are tableau lists (Theorem~4, p.~28, \texttt{full}).
Here is Theorem~1 (p.~19, \texttt{full}), with $\theta = \tfrac14$:

\Case{Suppose $\ISD$ is inconsistent:}
\begin{steps}
\item Then $\Prog{C}(\Prog{C})$ halts: there are $D$-proofs $j_1$ of a
  $\Pi_1^*$ sentence $\Phi$ and $j_2$ of a sentence $\theta$ with
  $\Pair(\gn{\Phi}, \gn{\theta})$. By \S\ref{sec:conventions}, $j_2$ is also a
  proof of $\Psi := \mathrm{dual}(\Phi)$, ``the $\Sigma_1^*$ theorem that is
  $\Phi$'s negation'' (p.~20). Choose the pair with $m = \max(j_1, j_2)$ as
  small as possible.
\item Groups 0--2 are true. Group-3 is false, but its first counterexample is
  a pair of proofs whose maximum is $m$, so it is App$\forall(m-1)$-valid.
  Hence $\ISD$ is Normed$(m-1, 1)$, which presupposes $m - 1 > 1$; under
  Willard's encoding every proof has Gödel number far above~3.
\item The proof $j_1 \le m$ certifies the universal $\Phi$ out to
  $(m-1)/m^{1/4}$. The proof $j_2 \le m$ finds a witness for the existential
  $\Psi$ at most $m^{1/4}$.
\item Since $(m-1)/m^{1/4} > m^{1/4}$, the witness for $\Psi$ lies where
  $\Phi$ has been certified.\contra
\end{steps}

\smallskip\noindent
The work is in the third arrow, $\theta$-compactification, and it is a
program. For Part~A of Theorem~2 (p.~26), suppose a tableau $p$ proves a
$\Sigma_1^*$ sentence $\Phi$ from a Normed$(a,b)$ system but $\Phi$ has no
witness $\le b\,p^{1/4}$:

\Case{Suppose $p$ is a tableau proof of $\Phi$ and $\Phi$ is not
App$\exists(b\,p^{1/4})$-valid:}
\begin{steps}
\item Then the root $\neg\Phi$ is App$\forall(b\,p^{1/4})$-valid, and the
  axioms together with the root form a Normed$(b\,p^{1/4}, b)$ system.
\item Run $\mathit{Probe}$ (Lemma~1, p.~23, \texttt{full}): walk down $p$ from
  the root; at a fork take a child that still satisfies the branch condition
  (each universal valid up to $b\,p^{1/4}$, each existential witnessed below
  $b$, each bounded sentence true) under the current valuation; when an existential is eliminated, give its new parameter the
  least value that keeps the branch true. Every parameter introduced at a node
  of U-Depth $\Delta$ gets a value at most $b \cdot 2^\Delta$ (Eq.~19), because
  terms grow at most by doubling.
\item The walk succeeds if the tree's U-Height is below
  $\log_2(b\,p^{1/4}) - \log_2 b = \tfrac14\log_2 p$, which Eq.~(20) guarantees.
\item A branch found by $\mathit{Probe}$ cannot close (Lemma~2, p.~25,
  \texttt{full}): a closing pair must be bounded sentences (footnote~3), and
  both would be true under the valuation.
\item So $p$ has an open branch, and is not a proof.\contra
\end{steps}

\smallskip\noindent
Part~B, for $\Pi_1^*$ sentences, permutes the quantifiers (Appendix~B,
pp.~40--41). Theorem~3, for tableaux, is then immediate, and Theorem~5, for
tableau lists, follows the same way from Theorem~4.

\emph{Why $\Pair$ is fixed as it is.} Theorem~1 is about a $\Pi_1^*$
sentence and its $\Sigma_1^*$ negation, because App$\exists$ validity is
defined for $\Sigma_1^*$ sentences (Definition~5). Willard's Eq.~(28),
though, pairs $f$ with the string $\neg f$. With an unspecified Prenex$^*$
rewriting, a proof of $\neg\Phi$ need not be a proof of
$\mathrm{dual}(\Phi)$, and Willard's ``If $\mathrm{IS}_D(A)$ is inconsistent,
then such a triple $(j_1, j_2, m)$ will clearly exist'' (p.~20, text layer)
would pass over that step. The convention and the $\Pair$ of
\S\ref{sec:conventions} close it: every sentence $\Pair$ accepts has exactly
the tableau proofs of $\mathrm{dual}(\Phi)$.

Willard's earlier intuition for the 2001 system, his ``Assertion ++'', is the
same horizon argument seen from the other side (2001, Remark~4.4, p.~20,
verbatim): ``any proof $y$ of $0=1$ from $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ will be
automatically associated with an ordered pair $(p,u)$ where
$p \le \mathrm{VALUE}(u) < y$ and $p$ represents ANOTHER proof of $0=1$''. He
calls this a \emph{vacuous truth}, an implication whose hypothesis is never
satisfied. The 2001 proof replaces it by an eleven-case induction showing that
the least tableau proof $y$ of $0=1$ contains a branch on which every sentence
is true once its unbounded quantifiers are cut off at $y-1$ (Lemma~4.8,
p.~23, \texttt{full}), which cannot close (Lemma~4.6, p.~22, \texttt{full}).
The Group-3 axiom is false, but its cut-off at $y-1$ is true, because $y$ is
the least refutation. The naming bounds are Lemma~4.1 (a constant mentioned
in $y$ has value below $\sqrt y$, p.~17, \texttt{full}) and Lemma~4.2 (along
such a branch every parameter has value below $y$, p.~19, \texttt{sketch}),
whose intuition is that fewer than $\tfrac13\log_2 y$ doublings take values
at most to $y^{1/2} \cdot y^{1/3} = y^{5/6} < y$ (the product is our
unpacking of the sketch). The formal proof uses a different majorant, Part~II
of the affirmative-branch definition, $\mathrm{VALUE}(u) < (k + \sqrt y)\cdot
2^{\lfloor \beta_i/6\rfloor - 2}$ (Eq.~26, p.~22), where $\beta_i$ is the
bit-length of the first $i$ levels of the branch and each doubling is paid
for by the six bits of Case~11. Willard later found this
proof ``hard to visualize'' and replaced it by Theorem~1 (2005, Remark~2,
p.~21).

Three remarks.

\emph{The proof is a program.} $\mathit{Probe}$ takes any purported proof and
returns an open branch together with values for its parameters. Willard's
consistency proof is therefore a machine that defeats each refutation by
running it, in a universe barely larger than the refutation itself. That is
self-justification ex machina in the most literal sense. (And it is why we
named the searcher $\Prog{C}$: it hunts for a tree all of whose branches
close, and the walk always finds one that stays open.) $\F$ does not prove
that the machine works; that is the external part. $\F$ has only the axiom.

\emph{The encoding enters as a floor.} The horizon is measured in Gödel
numbers, so the argument needs every symbol to cost bits: Eq.~(20) requires
on average more than five bits per U-Grounding function symbol along the
deepest branch, and 2001's Case~11 needs the sentence introducing a sum to require
``substantially more than six bits'' (2001, p.~28). Larger Gödel numbers only
widen the margin, and Willard says so: ``All our theorems would also be valid
for uncompressed encoding methods. However, they are more meaningful when
one uses an efficiently compressed B-adic encoding method'' (2005, p.~40,
text layer). 2006a makes the analogous remark for the Hilbert line: its
theorems ``have analogs under such uncompressed encoding methods, but they are
substantially more meaningful'' when compressed (Appendix~A, p.~37, text
layer). What would break the 2005 proof of Theorem~2 is a notation
\emph{more} compact than its argument allows. Part~A needs
$\text{U-Height}(p) < \tfrac14 \log_2 p$ (footnote~5, p.~27), so the third
arrow fails once there are on average four or fewer bits per U-Grounding
function symbol along the deepest branch. Whether Theorem~3 itself fails for
such an encoding Willard does not say, and we do not know.

\emph{The closing inequality.} Willard closes the proof of Theorem~1 with
``since $\theta < \tfrac13$ implies $g > h$'', where $g = (m-1)/m^\theta$ and
$h = m^\theta$ (p.~20). That needs $m - 1 > m^{2\theta}$, which holds for every
$\theta < \tfrac13$ once $m \ge 4$, and for $\theta = \tfrac14$ once $m \ge 3$.
Under Appendix~A's encoding every proof has Gödel number above $32 \cdot 64^2$,
so the gap is harmless; Theorems~3 and~5, which are corollaries of
Theorem~1, inherit both the gap and its repair.

\section{The Faith Is Not Just Missing}
\label{sec:backwards}

One might think Willard's systems are self-justifying merely because nobody
has managed to prove step (2) in them. In fact they cannot prove it, and the
reason is Oberhoff's Theorem~6 run backwards.

\begin{proposition}[ours]\label{prop:backwards}
Let $(\F, D)$ be a configuration with $D$ $\F$-sound and $\F$-complete, and
$\Prf^D_{\F}$ a bounded formula that is correct in the standard model (it
holds of $(h, x)$ exactly when $x$ codes a $D$-proof from $\F$ of the
sentence coded $h$), with $\Subst$ likewise correct. Let $\G$, $\mathsf{H}$,
$c$ and $c'$ be as in \S\ref{sec:conventions}. Suppose:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(H\arabic*), leftmargin=3em]
\item $\F$ is Level(0$-$) consistent: there is no $D$-proof of $0=1$ from
  $\F$;
\item $(\F, D)$ watches;
\item $\F$ proves the true bounded sentences and trichotomy at $\bar c$, so
  that (FP) holds (Fact~\ref{fact:fp});
\item $\F$ proves its own Level(1) consistency,
  $\F \vdash_D \forall x\,\forall y\,\forall p\,\forall q\;
  \neg[\Pair(x,y) \wedge \Prf^D_{\F}(x,p) \wedge \Prf^D_{\F}(y,q)]$, for a
  bounded $\Pair$ with $\Pair(\bar c, \bar c\,')$ true.
\end{enumerate}
Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\F \vdash_D (2) \leftrightarrow \G$ and
  $\F \vdash_D [\mathsf{H} \supset \Prov(\bar c\,')] \leftrightarrow \G$;
\item $\F \nvdash_D \G$;
\item hence $\F$ proves neither step (2) nor the instance of knowing that it
  watches at the $\Sigma_1$-class sentence $\mathsf{H}$, and both are true.
\end{enumerate}
Part~(b) uses only (H1), (H2), $\F \vdash_D \Subst(\bar n, \bar c)$, the
correctness of $\Prf^D_\F$, and the soundness and completeness of $D$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) By (H3), $\F$ proves the true bounded sentence $\Pair(\bar c, \bar c\,')$.
With (H4), Fact~\ref{fact:closure} gives step (3): $\F \vdash_D
\neg[\Prov(\bar c) \wedge \Prov(\bar c\,')]$. Now take the two directions.
Step (3) and (FP) together imply $(2) \supset \G$ in every model: from (2)
and (3), $\neg\Prov(\bar c)$, and then (FP) gives $\G$. (FP) alone implies
$\G \supset (2)$ in every model: $\G$ gives $\neg\Prov(\bar c)$, which makes
the antecedent of (2) false. Since $\F$ proves (3) and (FP),
Fact~\ref{fact:closure}, applied with these two implications as its
conclusion $\chi$, gives $\F \vdash_D (2) \leftrightarrow \G$. The second
equivalence follows by Lemma~\ref{lem:instances}(b).

(b) This is Oberhoff's \S4, case 1:
\begin{topsteps}
\item Suppose $\F \vdash_D \G$, by a proof $m$.
\item Since $\Prf^D_\F$ is correct, $\Prov(\bar c)$ is a true $\Sigma_1$-class
  sentence (with witness $m$), so $\F \vdash_D \Prov(\bar c)$ by (H2): $\F$
  watches $\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ halt.
\item By (FP$\Rightarrow$) and Fact~\ref{fact:closure}, $\F \vdash_D \neg\G$.
  Then $\F$ proves both $\G$ and $\neg\G$, and Fact~\ref{fact:closure} gives
  $\F \vdash_D 0=1$, against (H1).\contra
\end{topsteps}

(c) By (a) and (b). Truth: since no proof of $\G$ exists and $\Prf^D_\F$ and
$\Subst$ are correct, $\G$ is true in the standard model, step (2) is true
because its antecedent is false, and the instance is true because
$\mathsf{H}$ is false.
\end{proof}

\noindent
In a system that meets these hypotheses, then, the faith step \emph{is} the
Gödel sentence, up to provable equivalence, and is true and unprovable for
the same reason. It fails at a $\Sigma_1$-class sentence that says
$\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ halts, and at every sentence with the same tableau
proofs, $\neg\G$ among them, since $\Pair$ accepts those too. A $\Sigma_1$
formalization of the halting statement with \emph{different} tableau proofs,
such as $\exists x\,\exists h\,[\Subst(\bar n, h) \wedge \Prf(h, x)]$, is a
different sentence, because $\Prov$ is sensitive to codes. The proposition
says nothing about it, and the essay's claims do not need it.

\begin{corollary}[ours]\label{cor:isd}
Let $A$ prove every $\Pi_1^*$ theorem of $\mathrm{PA}{+}$, let every $\Pi_1^*$
theorem of $A$ be true, and let $D$ be semantic tableaux or
$\mathrm{Tab}{-}U_1^*{-}\mathrm{List}$. Take $\Prf^D_{\ISD}$ to be Willard's
$\mathit{SubstPrf}^D_{\mathrm{UNION}(A)}(\bar n_3, \cdot, \cdot)$, with
$\Pair$, the Prenex$^*$ convention and the encoding as fixed in
\S\ref{sec:conventions}, and $\G$ and $\mathsf{H}$ built from it. Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\ISD$ does not prove $\G$;
\item $\ISD$ proves neither step (2) nor the instance of knowing that it
  watches at $\mathsf{H}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
\emph{The proof predicate.} Willard's Eq.~(7) is $\Delta_0^*$ (2005, p.~10),
so $\Prf^D_{\ISD}$ is bounded, and it is correct by
Lemma~\ref{lem:honest}(a).

\emph{Group-2 detachment.} If $A$ proves a $\Pi_1^*$ sentence $\Phi$ by a
proof $p$, then Groups 0 and 1 prove the true $\Delta_0^*$ sentence
$\Prf_A(\gn{\Phi}, \bar p)$, and with $\Phi$'s Group-2 axiom
$\forall p\,\{\Prf_A(\gn{\Phi}, p) \to \Phi\}$ (Eq.~5),
Fact~\ref{fact:closure} gives $\ISD \vdash_D \Phi$.

\emph{The hypotheses.} (H1): by Theorem~\ref{thm:willard}(a), Group-3 is
true, so no $\Pi_1^*$ sentence and its $\Sigma_1^*$ negation both have
proofs; a $D$-proof of $0=1$ would, by Fact~\ref{fact:closure}, yield proofs
of every sentence, including such a pair. (H2) is Lemma~\ref{lem:watch}.
(H3): Groups 0 and 1 prove the true bounded sentences, $\Subst(\bar n, \bar
c)$ and $\Pair(\bar c, \bar c\,')$ among them. Trichotomy is a $\Pi_1$
theorem of Peano Arithmetic, and so a $\Pi_1^*$ theorem of $\mathrm{PA}{+}$,
which detachment hands to $\ISD$. (H4) is literally the Group-3 axiom,
Eq.~(8). Part (a) is Proposition~\ref{prop:backwards}(b), part (b) is
Proposition~\ref{prop:backwards}(c).
\end{proof}

\noindent
Nothing in this corollary is assumed beyond what is cited: Willard's
consistency theorem and arithmetization, with their stated statuses, and the
conventions fixed in \S\ref{sec:conventions}. $\ISD$ is never asked to prove
that its Group-3 formula is its own proof predicate; that correctness is used
in the metatheory only.

\begin{corollary}[ours]\label{cor:is}
Let $A \supseteq \mathrm{PAX}$ be regularly consistent and $D$ semantic
tableaux, and let $\G$ and $\mathsf{H}$ be built from the proof predicate of
$\mathrm{IS}(A)$'s Group-3 axiom, with the conventions of
\S\ref{sec:conventions}. Then $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ does not prove $\G$, and does
not prove both step (2) and the Level(0$-$) form of step (3),
$\forall p\,\forall q\,[\Prf(\bar c, p) \wedge \Prf(\bar c\,', q) \supset
\exists r\,\Prf(\gn{0=1}, r)]$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:backwards} other than (H4) hold in
the setting of Willard's own Theorem~7.2. (H1) is
Theorem~\ref{thm:willard}(b). (H2): $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ proves every true
$\Delta_0^-$ sentence (2001, p.~39) and names every number by a constant, so
for a true $\Sigma_1^-$ sentence it proves the true $\Delta_0^-$ instance at
the witnesses, and Fact~\ref{fact:closure} adds the existential quantifiers.
(H3): $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ proves the true $\Delta_0^-$ sentences, among them
$\Subst(\bar n, \bar c)$ (2001, p.~39), and trichotomy is a $\Pi_1^-$ theorem
of $\mathrm{PAX}$, inherited through Group-2. The proof predicate is correct
by Lemma~\ref{lem:honest}(b). So
Proposition~\ref{prop:backwards}(b) gives $\mathrm{IS}(A) \nvdash \G$. If
$\mathrm{IS}(A)$ proved both step (2) and the combination sentence, then with
its Group-3 axiom $\forall r\,\neg\Prf(\gn{0=1}, r)$ it would prove step
(3), $\neg[\Prov(\bar c) \wedge \Prov(\bar c\,')]$, which is all the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:backwards}(a) uses of (H4). That proof would then give
$\mathrm{IS}(A) \vdash \G$.\contra
\end{proof}

\noindent
So for the 2001 system the closed faith step fails in at least one of two
places, and we do not know which. Its uniform form fails outright
(Corollary~\ref{cor:uniform}).

The proposition is a contrapositive and claims no novelty of method. Its
sibling in Willard is 2001 Theorem~7.2 (p.~38, \texttt{full}): no consistent
$\alpha$ with a $\Delta_0^-$ proof predicate, an $\alpha$-sound and
$\alpha$-complete $D$, and all $\Pi_1^-$ theorems of $\mathrm{PAX}$ can prove
the canonical reflection principle $\forall y\,\{\Prf^D_\alpha(\gn{\Upsilon}, y)
\supset \Upsilon\}$ for every $\Pi_1^-$ sentence $\Upsilon$. Its proof uses the
same fixed point and the same Fact~\ref{fact:closure} in place of the
derivability conditions; ``The above result is different from Löb's Theorem
\ldots{} chiefly because we do not assume that $\alpha$ recognizes either
Addition or Multiplication as total functions'' (p.~38, text layer).
Willard's 1994 framing already sets out the alternatives: ``every axiom system
either fails to prove its consistency or fails to meet the full
Hilbert-Bernays conditions'' (TR 93-10, p.~6, verbatim). What the
computational reading adds is \emph{which} condition fails, at \emph{which}
sentence, and that the failing instance is the Gödel sentence in disguise.
What Willard adds, and no contrapositive can supply, is that systems on the
far side of the alternative \emph{exist} (Theorem~\ref{thm:willard}).

\section{The Faith, Bought: Willard's Theorem 6}
\label{sec:bought}

Willard's 2005 paper ends by doing the experiment. Take the transcript
statement of \S\ref{sec:transcripts}, bound it, and see what it costs to
switch it on.

Let $\mathrm{Log}^k$ be the $k$-fold base-2 logarithm, rounded down, and
$\Upsilon(k,m)$ the true $\Sigma_1^*$ sentence $\exists z\; \mathrm{Log}^k(z) \ge
\bar m$, which says that a number at least $2^m_k$ exists, where $2^m_k$, the
least $z$ with $\mathrm{Log}^k(z) \ge m$, is roughly a tower of $k$
exponentials topped by $m$ (2005, Definition~2, p.~11). $\mathrm{NS}^{k,m}_D(A)$ is $\ISD$ with
$\Upsilon(k,m)$ added to Group-0 and Group-3's ``this'' adjusted to the larger
system (Definition~3, p.~11). Both systems prove $\Upsilon(k,m)$; only one of
them has it as an axiom. Let $\Mho(s,b)$ be the bounded Gödel sentence ``no
proof $p$ of me with $\mathrm{Log}^s(p) < b$'' (Eq.~24, p.~33), and
$\mathit{CheckProof}(t,p,q)$ the $\Delta_0^*$ formula ``$p$ proves $t$, and
$q$ proves $\exists p\,\Prf(t,p)$'' (Definition~6, p.~33). Lemma~4 (p.~33,
\texttt{full}, resting on Lemma~3, a proof \texttt{sketch} that checking a
proof is quasi-polynomially cheap) says that for $k$ large Peano Arithmetic
proves the $\Pi_1^*$ sentence (Willard writes Peano Arithmetic; the sentence is
in the U-Grounding language, so the theory meant is $\mathrm{PA}{+}$)
\begin{multline*}
\forall p\,\forall n\,\exists q < n\,\bigl\{[\mathrm{Log}^{k-1} p < m \wedge
\mathrm{Log}^k n \ge m \wedge \Prf(\gn{\Mho(k-1,m)}, p)] \\ \to
\mathit{CheckProof}(\gn{\Mho(k-1,m)}, p, q)\bigr\}. \tag{27}
\end{multline*}
This is the literal transcript of step (2), bounded. For every short proof
$p$ of the Gödel sentence and every number $n$ that is big enough, a
transcript $q$ exists below $n$, and $q$ proves the $\Sigma_1^*$ sentence
(25), $\exists p\,\Prf(\gn{\Mho(k-1,m)}, p)$: ``$\Mho$ is provable''. Unlike
the halting sentence for $\G$, this is not equivalent to the negation of the
Gödel sentence. $\Mho$ speaks only of short proofs, so $\neg\Mho$ says that a
short proof exists, while (25) says that some proof exists. Because the
transcript is bounded by $n$, the statement is
$\Pi_1^*$, and the system inherits it from $\mathrm{PA}{+}$ through Group-2.
What is missing is $n$. (Willard states Lemma~4 for the proof predicate of
$\mathrm{NS}^{k,m}_D(\mathrm{PA}{+})$. Lemma~3's sketch is generic in the
axiom system: the cited literature treats ``an axiom system $\alpha$'', and
``an analogous construction'' is to apply it to $\mathrm{NS}$ (p.~33). Applied
to $\mathrm{IS}_D(\mathrm{PA}{+})$, the same construction gives the bounded
transcript statement for that system's own proof predicate, with the same
status, \texttt{sketch}. Willard does not state it.)

\begin{theorem}[Willard 2005, Theorem~6, p.~34; \texttt{full} as printed, a \texttt{sketch} at this granularity, as explained below]
For $D = \mathrm{Tab}{-}U_1^*{-}\mathrm{List}$ there is $k_0$ such that for
every $k > k_0$, $\mathrm{NS}^{k,m}_D(\mathrm{PA}{+})$ is inconsistent.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Willard's proof is a seven-step tableau list $W$ (pp.~34--36), and, with
its fourth step repaired as sketched below, it is Oberhoff's Theorem~6 with
the faith supplied:

\begin{topsteps}
\item (27), inherited from Peano Arithmetic: the faith step, below $n$.
\item (28) $\Pair(\gn{\Mho}, \gn{\neg\Mho})$, a true bounded sentence; with
  $\Pair$ as fixed in \S\ref{sec:conventions}, also
  $\Pair(\gn{\Mho}, \gn{\mathrm{dual}(\Mho)})$.
\item (29) the Group-3 axiom, and (30) its instance: step (3).
\item (31), a step Willard calls ``mostly notational'': no $p$, $q$ satisfy
  $\mathit{CheckProof}(\gn{\Mho}, p, q)$. Then (32), chaining with (27): no
  short proof of $\Mho$ coexists with a big $n$.
\item (33) $\Upsilon(k,m)$, an axiom, supplies $n$: no short proof of $\Mho$
  exists. This is $\Mho(k-1,m)$ itself, which the seventh step derives.
\item Because $\Upsilon(k,m)$ cost nothing, $W$ is short: for $k > k_0$ its
  Gödel number is below $2^m_{k-1}$, so $W$ is itself one of the short proofs
  that $\Mho(k-1,m)$ says do not exist. The system watches $W$ and refutes
  $\Mho$.\contra
\end{topsteps}

\noindent
Willard's fourth step meets the distinction drawn after
Proposition~\ref{prop:backwards}. He derives (31) from (30) on the ground
that ``the definitions of CheckProof and $\Mho(k-1,m)$ imply that
CheckProof$(\gn{\Mho}, p, q)$ is logically equivalent to Equation (30)'s
curly bracket expression'' (p.~35, text layer), that is, to
$\Prf(\gn{\Mho}, p) \wedge \Prf(\gn{\neg\Mho}, q)$. By Definition~6, as
printed (we checked the page image), $q$ proves (25), which is no sentence
that $\Pair$ pairs with $\Mho$. (25) has neither the substitution nor the
bound $\mathrm{Log}^{k-1}(p) < m$, and a long proof of $\Mho$ makes (25)
true and $\neg\Mho$ false. No reading of Willard's text removes this, so the
step needs a repair.

\emph{The repair (ours, a sketch).} Replace (28) by the true bounded
sentence $\Pair(\gn{\Mho}, \gn{\mathrm{dual}(\Mho)})$, which $\Pair$ as fixed
in \S\ref{sec:conventions} accepts. With (29) it gives
$\forall p\,\forall q\,\neg[\Prf(\gn{\Mho}, p) \wedge
\Prf(\gn{\mathrm{dual}(\Mho)}, q)]$ in place of (30). Then redefine
$\mathit{CheckProof}(t, p, q)$ so that $q$ proves $\mathrm{dual}(\Mho)$. Now
(31) follows from the new (30) by pure logic, since $\mathit{CheckProof}$ is
literally its bracket. What remains is to show that such a $q$ is short.
Given a proof $p$ of $\Mho = \Mho(k-1,m)$ with $\mathrm{Log}^{k-1}(p) < m$, a
$q$ can be built as a tableau list of four parts:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), leftmargin=2.5em]
\item a proof of the bounded sentence $\Prf(\gn{\Mho}, \bar p)$, which is
  the object Lemma~3's sketch constructs: ``$q$ is a proof that $p$ is a
  $M$-encoded proof'' (p.~33);
\item a proof of the bounded sentence $\mathrm{Log}^{k-1}(\bar p) < \bar m$,
  which evaluates a closed term built from the non-growth function
  $\mathit{Logarithm}$;
\item a proof of the true bounded sentence $\Subst(\bar n, \gn{\Mho})$, which
  depends only on $k$ and $m$;
\item a tableau whose root, $\mathrm{dual}(\mathrm{dual}(\Mho))$, is the
  Prenex$^*$ form of $\neg\,\mathrm{dual}(\Mho)$. It instantiates the two
  universal quantifiers at $\bar p$ and $\gn{\Mho}$ (rule~7), removes the
  double negation (rule~2), branches (rules 2--4), and closes every branch
  against (i)--(iii). If (i)--(iii) are carried as $U_1^*$ intermediate
  theorems with a vacuous universal quantifier, as Willard's own bounded
  intermediate (28) must be, it instantiates those first (rule~7).
\end{enumerate}
Part (iv) mentions $\bar p$ and $\gn{\Mho}$ a bounded number of times. Also
$\gn{\Mho} < p$: $p$'s last root $\mathrm{dual}(\Mho)$ is a contiguous part
of $p$, one byte longer than $\Mho$ and beginning with a symbol byte, so by
the encoding of \S\ref{sec:conventions}, $\gn{\Mho} < 64^{|\Mho|} \le
\gn{\mathrm{dual}(\Mho)} \le p$. So the logarithm of (iv)'s Gödel number is
$O(\mathrm{Log}\, p)$. The repair then needs (i)--(iii) to be short enough
that Corollary~1's bound $F(p, t) < 2^b_a$ survives. Lemma~3's sketch
supplies (i). Parts (ii) and (iii) are claims of the same kind, about short
proofs of polynomial-time-checkable bounded facts, which the sketch does not
address. At this essay's granularity, then, Willard's printed proof of
Theorem~6 has a gap at step (31), and our repair is a sketch resting on
Lemma~3's sketch and on the length claims for (ii) and (iii). We count
Theorem~6 as a \texttt{sketch} here, although the registry records it as
\texttt{full}.

Willard calls the length estimate ``quite routine'' (p.~36). He measures the
difference between the two systems by a constant: proof lengths in
$\mathrm{NS}^{k,m}_D(A)$ and $\ISD$ differ by at most some $C > 2^m_k$, ``an
enormous quantity'' (p.~31). Why the argument does not transfer to $\ISD$ can
be seen with Willard's own engine (our observation). Under the hypothesis of
Theorem~\ref{thm:willard}(a) with $A = \mathrm{PA}{+}$, all axioms of
$\mathrm{IS}_D(\mathrm{PA}{+})$ are true $\Pi_1^*$ sentences: Group-3 by
Theorems~3 and~5. So the system is Normed$(a,1)$ for every $a > 1$.
$\mathrm{IS}_D(\mathrm{PA}{+})$ must prove $\Upsilon(k,m)$ as a lemma. Let $p >
1$ be a proof of it, and take $a = p$. Then $p \le \lceil a/1\rceil + 1$, and
$\theta$-compactification with $\theta = \tfrac14$ (Theorems~2 and~4) makes
$\Upsilon(k,m)$ App$\exists(p^{1/4})$-valid: it has a witness at most
$p^{1/4}$. The least witness is $2^m_k$, so $p \ge (2^m_k)^4$. In a tableau
list, the components up to the lemma form a list proof of it, and they are a
prefix of the whole list's byte string, so the whole list has a Gödel number
at least $p$. That lies far beyond the bound $2^m_{k-1}$ of which
$\Mho(k-1,m)$ speaks. ``A Kleene-like
Group-3 axiom has a different connotation when it refers to a short proof,
referencing itself, than when it refers to a long such proof'' (p.~32, text
layer). The asymmetry is exact: adding a true $\Pi_1^*$ sentence instead of
the $\Sigma_1^*$ sentence $\Upsilon(k,m)$ preserves consistency (p.~37,
stated as a generalization of Theorem~5). A universal axiom constrains; an
existential axiom supplies a number, and the number is what the faith step
was missing. Remark~8 (p.~36, \texttt{stated-only}) extends the theorem to
semantic tableaux and other cut-free methods, to empty $A$, and to systems
that drop the two Group-zero axioms declaring addition and doubling total.

\section{The Price of Faith}
\label{sec:price}

If withholding faith is what lets $\W$ survive, the second incompleteness
theorem should return as soon as the system can afford the transcripts.
Willard spent the second half of his career proving that it does. The price
depends on the language and on how much the proof system may reuse.

\subsection*{Squaring}

Give a tableau system multiplication as a total function. Then a proof can
introduce $u_0 = 2$, $u_{i+1} = u_i \cdot u_i$, and so reach $u_n = 2^{2^n}$, in
$O(n)$ steps. The 2001 system with multiplication added, $\mathrm{ISMULT}(A)$,
``satisfies an analogue of'' Assertion ++ but has no analogue of Lemma~4.2,
for exactly this reason (2001, Remark~4.5, p.~21). On the negative side
Willard located the failure precisely (2000, \S5, quoted in the codified
statement~\S5.3):
\begin{quote}\small
Many readers will smile with amusement when they learn the true
reason that the Semantic Tableaux version of the Second Incompleteness
Theorem breaks down when Multiplication is changed from a total function into
a 3-way relation. It is essentially that Lemma 8 and its short proof
then become no longer valid. \ldots{} Without it, our proof of the Semantic
Tableaux version of the Second Incompleteness Theorem collapses entirely!
\end{quote}
Lemma~8 (2000, p.~11, \texttt{full}) is the squaring construction: a tableau
proof from $Q + V$, of length $O([\log\log z]^C)$, that a number about as
large as $z$ exists. With it,
the second theorem comes back, at Level(0$-$):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item no consistent \emph{finite} extension of $Q + V$, for an explicit
  $\Pi_1$ sentence $V$ with $Q + V$ consistent, proves its own semantic
  tableaux consistency (2002a, Theorems~3.4--3.5, p.~11, \texttt{full});
\item every consistent extension of $T$-1 or $T$-2, the textbook and
  Wilkie--Paris axiomatizations of $I\Sigma_0$, is unable to prove its own
  semantic tableaux consistency (2007, Theorem~6, p.~29, \texttt{full});
\item any consistent extension of $I\Sigma_0$ with the Conventional
  Deciphering Property (a finite subset proves, of each axiom's canonical
  numeral, that it is an axiom: 2002a, Definition~5.1, p.~20) cannot prove
  that there is no tableau proof of $0=1$ from itself (2002a, Theorem~6.4,
  p.~25, \texttt{sketch}).
\end{itemize}
In 1981 Paris and Wilkie asked whether $I\Sigma_0$ satisfies the second
incompleteness theorem for cut-free deduction. 2002a presented Theorem~6.4 as
an answer; 2007 records that the 2002 paper covered only the textbook
induction scheme (``which we did not examine in our year-2002 article'', of
the Wilkie--Paris scheme: 2007, abstract, text layer), and its Theorem~6
covers both.
On the positive side, Definition~5 becomes ``impossible to satisfy'' once
multiplication is admitted as a ninth function symbol (2005, Remark~4, p.~21,
\texttt{stated-only} as to the reason: ``intuitively because multiplication
has a faster growth property than addition'').\footnote{What multiplication
destroys is the flat consistency axiom and canonical reflection, not every
self-referential construction. Willard's $\mathrm{ISM}(A)$ recognizes
multiplication as total and is consistent whenever $A$ is regularly
consistent, ``by a routine generalization'' of his proof, but its self-referential axiom is weaker, and it cannot prove the
flat statement that no tableau proof of $0=1$ exists (2001, \S6, p.~36;
codified statement~\S7.2).}

\subsection*{Subroutines}

A Hilbert-style proof can prove a lemma once and use it many times. Modus
ponens is a subroutine call. A tableau cannot do this; every use must be
inlined. Inlining can be astronomically expensive: by Gentzen, the tableau
proof exists, but ``$t$'s length can certainly be super-exponentially longer''
than the proofs it combines (2002a, Theorem~2.2, p.~7, \texttt{sketch}), and
Boolos gave a famous small example of the blowup~\cite{boolos84}. With
subroutines, a little growth is amplified into a lot. Willard saw this in 1994
(TR 93-10, p.~7, verbatim):
\begin{quote}\small
These metaphors lose meaning when cuts are present because Addition can then
be employed to \emph{implicitly define} a sequence that grows actually faster
than $a_0, a_1, a_2, a_3 \ldots$. Solovay's proof that IS(A) is incompatible
with deductive cuts [So94] rests on this fast growth property. (Actually,
IS(A) can support a cut rule for $\Delta_0$ sentences, and Solovay's theorem
relies on the cut rule for sentences with unbounded quantifiers.)
\end{quote}
The boundary depends on the \emph{complexity} of the lemmas, and Willard
mapped it:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item a cut rule for bounded ($\Delta_0$) sentences: compatible with
  $\mathrm{IS}(A)$, as the parenthesis above says (TR 93-10, p.~7; the
  codification records the construction in a footnote on p.~35, obligation
  O56);
\item lemmas with one unbounded quantifier block ($\Pi_1^*$ or $\Sigma_1^*$),
  addition total: self-justifying at Level(1) (2005, Theorem~5, \texttt{full});
\item lemmas with two blocks ($\Pi_2^*$, respectively $\Sigma_2^*$), addition
  total: for explicit $\Pi_1^*$ theorems $V_A$, $V_B$ of Peano Arithmetic, no
  consistent system in the U-Grounding language containing $V_A$ (resp.\ $V_B$)
  proves its own Level(0$-$) consistency under $\mathrm{Tab}{-}\Pi_2^*$
  (resp.\ $\mathrm{Tab}{-}\Sigma_2^*$) lists (2004, Theorem~2, p.~344,
  \texttt{sketch}; stated for finite systems ``for the sake of simplifying our
  proof'');
\item unrestricted lemmas (Hilbert deduction), successor total together
  with the $\Pi_1$ algebra of $+$ and $\times$: Solovay's theorem below.
\end{itemize}
The unrestricted case runs through the classical shortening of definable
cuts. In outline (a textbook gloss, not Willard's text): with lemmas, one
defines a set of numbers and proves once that it is closed under successor;
then, by a short proof reusing that lemma, that a smaller set is closed under
addition, and then that a smaller set still is closed under multiplication.
On the last set the system can afford transcripts, and Gödel's argument runs
there. Pudlák showed that a consistent extension of Robinson's $Q$ proves
neither its Hilbert consistency nor its consistency restricted to any
definable cut~\cite{pudlak85} (Willard 2006a, Theorem~1, p.~4, \texttt{cited}).
Solovay's theorem, communicated to Willard by telephone in April 1994 and
never published by Solovay, states the conclusion (2006a, Theorem~2, p.~4,
\texttt{cited}): a consistent system that formalizes arithmetic with
three-place predicates for $+$ and $\times$, proves successor total, and
proves the $\Pi_1$ associative, commutative, distributive and identity laws for
those predicates cannot prove that there is no Hilbert proof of $0=1$ from
itself. With unrestricted subroutines, the price of faith falls to successor
together with the ring laws for $+$ and $\times$ as relations: neither
addition nor multiplication need be total.

So the Hilbert-line self-justifying systems give up successor itself. They
name numbers with constants $C_1, C_2, \ldots$, and the naming convention
\emph{is} the system (codified statement~\S5.5). Incremental naming
$C_i = C_{i-1} + 1$ is how 2006a reads 2001's $\mathrm{ISREF}(A)$, which is
consistent when $A$ is regularly consistent (2001, Theorem~3.4, p.~13,
\texttt{full}) and whose Group-3 is the tangibility schema (2001, Eq.~10), not
a flat consistency axiom: ``The term `incremental naming convention' had not
appeared in our article [46]. However, its Theorem 3.4 had implicitly presumed
the presence of an incremental naming method'' (2006a, p.~22, text layer).
Additive naming $C_i = C_{i-1} + C_{i-1}$ works and keeps
reaching new numbers ($\mathrm{ISCE}(A)$, for consistent $A$ with true
$\Pi_1^-$ theorems and $\Delta_0^-$ Hilbert proof predicate: 2006a,
Theorem~3, p.~6, \texttt{full}); multiplicative naming
$C_i = C_{i-1} \cdot C_{i-1}$ fails for any system that contains the
convention's axioms, proves Peano Arithmetic's $\Pi_1^-$ theorems and has
Willard's Concise Encoding property (2006a, Theorem~4, p.~7, \texttt{full}).
Remark~3, quoted in \S\ref{sec:transcripts}, explains why.

\subsection*{Other ways to buy faith}

Excluded middle as a logical axiom, $\Phi \vee \neg\Phi$ at any node for any
$\Phi$ ($\mathrm{Xtab}$, 2020, p.~10), is what TR 93-10 had already defined as
``a tableaux proof with cuts'' (printed p.~33, as recorded in codified
statement~\S7.3). Then
$\mathrm{IS}_{\mathrm{Xtab}}(\beta)$ is automatically inconsistent whenever
$\beta$ proves the usual $\Pi_1^*$ associative, commutative, distributive and
identity laws for addition and multiplication (2020, Theorem~4.5, p.~12,
\texttt{sketch}). And letting $\W$ say more about itself brings the second
theorem back: there is a $\Pi_1^*$ theorem $W$ of Peano Arithmetic such that
no consistent finite system in the U-Grounding language containing $W$ can
prove its own Level($2^+$) tableaux consistency, the absence of proofs of a
Boolean combination of $\Pi_2^*$ and $\Sigma_2^*$ sentences together with its
negation (2004, Theorem~1, p.~342, \texttt{full}, although the codification
records that one step is deferred, in a footnote saying it ``should not be
considered a formal proof'': codified statement~\S7.5).

Read the table as Willard read it. Negative rows assert the weakest

sketches and one unpublished theorem. The boundary is sharply located in some

\section{Trust Within Reach}

A system \emph{trusts} its proofs if it proves canonical reflection,
$\forall y\,\{\Prf_\alpha(\gn{\Psi}, y) \supset \Psi\}$, for every $\Pi_1$-class
$\Psi$. That is too much to ask: 2001 Theorem~7.2 (\S\ref{sec:backwards})
forbids it for any consistent system with a bounded proof predicate that
inherits the $\Pi_1^-$ theorems of $\mathrm{PAX}$. What Willard's systems can
prove is \emph{tangibility reflection} (2001, Definition~1.2, p.~4, Eq.~2):
\[
\forall x\;\bigl\{\,[\exists y\, \Prf_\alpha(\gn{\Psi}, y)
\wedge \mathit{Tangible}(x)] \supset \Psi^{x}\,\bigr\},
\]
where $\Psi^x$ is $\Psi$ with its unbounded universal quantifiers bounded by
$x$, and $\mathit{Tangible}$ is one of $\mathit{TangPred}(x) \equiv \exists v\,
x < v - 1$, $\mathit{TangDiv}_k(x) \equiv \exists v\, x < v/k$, or
$\mathit{TangRoot}_k(x) \equiv \exists v\, x < v^{1/k}$. $\mathrm{ISREF}(A)$,
a Hilbert-style system without addition total, proves the $\mathit{TangPred}$
form for every prenex$^*$ $\Psi$, given that $\forall y\,\mathit{Size}(y) <
y - 1$ (2001, Theorem~3.5, p.~14, \texttt{full}). The tableau system
$\mathrm{IS}^\lambda(A)$ has a Hilbert proof of the $\mathit{TangRoot}_k$ form
when $0 < \lambda < 1$, $k > 1/(1-\lambda)$, and $A$ proves a stated $\Pi_1^-$
sentence (Theorem~6.1, p.~34, \texttt{full}), and a tableau proof by cut
elimination (Corollary~6.2, p.~35, \texttt{full}); its consistency is proved
for $\lambda = 3/4$ (Theorem~5.1, p.~28, \texttt{full}), where the condition
reads $k > 4$. In computational terms: \emph{I trust my verdicts on every
input for which there is memory to spare.} For a $\Sigma_1$-class claim the
bound costs nothing, since $\Psi \equiv \Psi^0$; taking $\Psi$ to be $0=1$
gives back the flat statement that no proof of $0=1$ exists (2001, p.~8).

\section{Taking Off the Glasses}

Oberhoff's \S5 takes the glasses off $\G$ and finds ``I am not provable''
underneath. Take them off $\W$ and you find ``nothing refutes me'', that is,
``I am consistent''. Over extensions of $I\Sigma_1$ with Hilbert deduction,
the metatheory shows that $\Fo + \W$ derives a contradiction
(Proposition~\ref{prop:genius}), so $\Prog{C}(\Prog{C})$ halts. In
Willard's systems that refutation would have to establish the existence of a
transcript-sized object from inside a proof smaller than the object. The
snake cannot swallow its tail, because its mouth is smaller than its body.

So self-reference is not where the trouble lies. The fixed point builds $\W$
in Willard's systems as easily as in Peano Arithmetic. What costs something is
\emph{self-simulation}: running yourself in your head and writing down the
result. The second incompleteness theorem needs both. The first needs
self-reference and watching, and, in Rosser's form, one $\Pi_1$ fact about a
loop. That is why Willard's systems obey it.

Oberhoff found that his classical and computational proofs were ``the exact
same''. The same holds here. Willard's arithmetic (a least pair of
contradictory proofs, values below $b \cdot 2^{\text{U-Depth}}$, five bits per
symbol) and the computational picture (a shortest refutation, the memory a
proof occupies, a walk down the tree) are one argument. The arithmetic of
Gödel numbers is the bookkeeping of memory.

\section{Refactoring the Incompleteness Theorems, Again}

Oberhoff's Theorem~7 is two theorems. Stated as properties of a
configuration $(\alpha, D)$, they are as follows. Property~7a is what this
essay takes from Oberhoff's Theorem~7. Property~7b, in both its closed and
its uniform form, is the essay's own formulation of what his Theorem~6
takes on faith.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{7a} (Watching, Definition~\ref{def:watch}). \emph{$\alpha \vdash_D
\sigma$ for every true $\Sigma_1$-class sentence $\sigma$; in particular, for
the halting sentence of each halting computation.}

\textbf{7b} (Knowing that one watches, Definition~\ref{def:know} and
\S\ref{sec:transcripts}). \emph{$\alpha \vdash_D \sigma \supset
\Prov^D_\alpha(\gn{\sigma})$ for every $\Sigma_1$-class sentence $\sigma$; and
uniformly, $\alpha \vdash_D \forall x\,[\delta(x) \supset \Prov^D_\alpha(\text{the
code of } \delta(\bar x))]$ for bounded $\delta$.}
\end{quote}

\noindent
Property~7a holds for every configuration in this essay: for extensions of
Robinson's $Q$ with Hilbert deduction (standard), for $\ISD$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:watch}), and for $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ with $A \supseteq
\mathrm{PAX}$ (\S\ref{sec:machine}). Property~7b, in both forms, holds for
recursively axiomatized extensions of $I\Sigma_1$ with their canonical
provability predicate~\cite{hp93}. Property~7a gives Oberhoff's Theorem~4 and
the $\G$ half of his \S4. Rosser's form also needs the $\Pi_1$ loop sentence
of footnote~12, which Willard's systems get from their base theory
(\S\ref{sec:machine}). Property~7b is what Theorem~6 takes on faith, and it
does not follow from~7a:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item In its uniform form, 7b fails for both of Willard's systems, under the
  hypotheses of their consistency theorems: they cannot prove even that every
  number has a written name (Corollary~\ref{cor:uniform}).
\item At a closed sentence, 7b fails for $\ISD$ under the hypotheses of
  Corollary~\ref{cor:isd}. The sentence is $\mathsf{H}$, the $\Sigma_1$
  statement that $\Prog{P}(\Prog{P})$ halts, and the failing instance is
  provably equivalent to the Gödel sentence
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:backwards}).
\item For $\mathrm{IS}(A)$, under the hypotheses of Corollary~\ref{cor:is},
  either step (2), and with it 7b at $\mathsf{H}$, fails, or the Level(0$-$)
  form of step (3) does. We do not know which.
\end{itemize}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:instances}(a) these systems satisfy~7b at every
\emph{true} $\Sigma_1$-class sentence, so the closed failure is confined to a
run that does not happen. The uniform failure is about writing numbers down,
which costs more than linearly many bits. Willard's Theorem~6
(\S\ref{sec:bought}) shows a bounded transcript form of~7b, at ``$\Mho$ is
provable'', to be a $\Pi_1^*$ theorem of $\mathrm{PA}{+}$, inherited through
Group-2. With Definition~6 repaired as sketched there, its transcript
reaches the sentence $\Pair$ pairs with $\Mho$. What the system lacks is one
number large
enough to switch it on, and granting that number as an axiom makes the system
inconsistent.

So Gödel's work splits into three parts, not two. The first is programming
the integers (7a). The second is teaching the integers that they have been
programmed (7b). The third is the victory laps. Willard's self-justifying
systems live in the gap between the first and the second. The second
incompleteness theorem is a victory lap only for a runner who has already
paid for~7b. Willard's accounting does not give one price. It gives four
boundaries (\S\ref{sec:price}):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item tableaux without lemmas, multiplication total: the second theorem holds
  at Level(0$-$) for consistent finite extensions of $Q + V$ and for
  consistent extensions of $I\Sigma_0$'s two axiomatizations (2002a,
  Theorem~3.5; 2007, Theorem~6; \texttt{full});
\item lemmas with one unbounded quantifier block, addition total and
  multiplication a relation: $\ISD$ is self-justifying at Level(1), and
  consistent whenever $A$'s $\Pi_1^*$ theorems are true (2005, Theorem~5,
  \texttt{full});
\item lemmas with two blocks, addition total: for explicit $\Pi_1^*$ theorems
  $V_A$, $V_B$ of Peano Arithmetic, no consistent finite system containing
  $V_A$ (respectively $V_B$) proves its own Level(0$-$) consistency under
  $\Pi_2^*$ (respectively $\Sigma_2^*$) lemma lists (2004, Theorem~2,
  \texttt{sketch}; finiteness ``for the sake of simplifying our proof'');
\item unrestricted lemmas: successor total together with the $\Pi_1$ ring
  laws for $+$ and $\times$ as relations already suffices (Solovay,
  \texttt{cited}).
\end{itemize}
At the finest grain, one large number granted as an axiom is enough (2005,
Theorem~6, a \texttt{sketch} at this granularity).

\medskip
Moral of the story: you may declare yourself a very stable genius, and
Willard's theorems will vouch for you provided your teachers told you only
truths, you cannot multiply, and you reuse only lemmas with one unbounded
quantifier block. Learn to multiply, and (for the theories Willard treats)
even proofs without lemmas are too much. Reuse lemmas with two blocks, and
(by a sketch of Willard's) addition is already too much. Reuse any lemma you
like, and (by Solovay's unpublished theorem) successor with the ring laws for
$+$ and $\times$ is too much. And you must never be told, as an axiom, that a
big enough number exists.

\section{What Self-Justification Does Not Buy}

Willard was more careful about the limits of his results than most of his
readers have been, and an honest retelling must carry his caveats.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \textbf{Relative consistency.} Theorem~\ref{thm:willard} assumes that
  the base theory's $\Pi_1$-class theorems are true. Nothing here shows any
  system consistent outright; the systems are as trustworthy as their base.
  Willard: ``if $A$ = Peano Arithmetic, IS(A) can prove all the $\Pi_1^-$
  theorems of Peano Arithmetic, and its consistency will be as certain as
  that of Peano Arithmetic!'' (2001, p.~11, text layer).
\item \textbf{A reduced question.} Willard (2016, Remark~4.9, p.~13) splits
  Hilbert's question in two: can a system verify its own consistency in a
  robust sense, and ``Can logic systems `appreciate' their own consistency in
  some REDUCED sense, that is diluted but not fully immaterial?'' (text
  layer). The answer to the first is no, by Gödel, Hilbert--Bernays
  and Pudlák--Solovay. Everything above answers the second.
\item \textbf{A one-line proof.} A system that proves its consistency from
  its own axiom $\W$ has ``essentially a 1-line proof'' (2011, Remark~6.16,
  p.~28), which is instinctive faith rather than proof-justification. The
  substantive justification lies outside: the theorem that $\mathit{Probe}$
  defeats every refutation, proved in the metatheory under the hypothesis on
  $A$.
\item \textbf{Almost cheating.} Willard raised the objection against
  himself: ``Is it not almost cheating when an axiom system verifies its own
  consistency by using $\oplus$'s formalized `I am consistent' axiom as an
  intermediate step, to verify its own consistency? After all, such a
  technique can verify its own consistency only in a technically purely
  legalistic sense'' (2016, \S8). His answer, \emph{Platonic Stability}, is
  that a thinking being needs no sophisticated justification of its own
  reasoning, only a formalism in which presuming its own consistency provably
  will not spin it into inconsistency. Its formal statement, Corollary~8.2
  (p.~28), opens ``Let us assume Conjecture 6.6 is correct (as we are almost
  100\,\% certain it is)''; Conjecture~6.6 (p.~23) is \texttt{stated-only}.
\item \textbf{The first theorem stands.} Under the hypothesis of
  Theorem~\ref{thm:willard}(a), with $A$ proving the $\Pi_1^*$ theorems of
  $\mathrm{PA}{+}$, $\ISD$ decides neither $\G$ nor $\R$. $\G$ is unprovable
  by Corollary~\ref{cor:isd}(a), $\neg\G$ by honesty
  (Lemma~\ref{lem:honest}(a)), and Rosser's form holds by
  \S\ref{sec:machine}. The same holds for $\mathrm{IS}(A)$ with $A \supseteq
  \mathrm{PAX}$ regularly consistent (Corollary~\ref{cor:is},
  Lemma~\ref{lem:honest}(b)), as Willard said in 1994: it satisfies
  ``Godel's First but not Second Incompleteness Theorem'' (TR 93-10, p.~6).
\item \textbf{Interpretation versus source.} The vocabulary of watching and
  knowing, the conventions of \S\ref{sec:conventions} (the least-witness
  substitution, the unbounded fixed point, the Prenex$^*$ rewriting, $\Pair$
  and the numbering of sentences), Proposition~\ref{prop:herbrand} and its
  corollary, the program
  $\Prog{C}$ as a reading of Willard's Group-3, the horizon picture,
  Lemmas~\ref{lem:instances}--\ref{lem:honest},
  Proposition~\ref{prop:backwards} and its corollaries, the reading of
  Willard's Theorem~6 as Oberhoff's Theorem~6 with the faith supplied, and the
  repair of its fourth step are ours, not Willard's formulations. His own words for the mechanism are
  ``adequate memory'' (1994) and the comparison, in 2006a Remark~3, between a
  proof's Gödel number and the integer the proof shows to exist.
\end{itemize}

\medskip\noindent
{\small (The cake promised in Oberhoff's abstract is still on its way. We
cannot prove that it exists: it is one multiplication away.)}
